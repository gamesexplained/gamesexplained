About Games Explained

Every game’s internals, explained from the code, in one place, by everyone. It’s meant to be Wikipedia for how games actually work, with a kit for building each game its own minisite.

Who runs it

I’m Aaron Bell, air on GitHub. I’m an engineer and a lifelong Commodore 64 fan.

In 1991 I was fourteen, with a brown breadbox 64, and I read a caption in Commodore Format that stuck with me for years: “You can’t get this colour on a 64! Well, you can if you swap pale green and cyan 50 times a second!” Twenty-six years later I found out what those colours look like, and wrote it up with interactive examples as Secret colours of the Commodore 64. It reached the front page of Hacker News in 2017, and again in 2023.

Between 2012 and 2017 I rebuilt Encounter, Paul Woakes’s 1984 game for Novagen, in the browser. I wrote it by hand in WebGL, long before coding agents, and read some of its sound frequencies straight out of the C64’s memory. You can play it; the devlogs and the source are public too. This site’s Encounter page explains the original from its code.

I built the kit this site runs on, working with Claude, and took the first eight games through it myself. My rules for running the site: review fast, be generous with credit, and never claim a game I haven’t done.

Why I started it

I started Games Explained in September 2026. Coding agents had made it possible for anyone to reverse engineer their favourite games, and enthusiasts were publishing write-ups all over the web. Some of them are great. But they’re scattered and hard to find, and every project starts from scratch, rediscovering the same tricks.

The site sets out to supply what was missing: somewhere to find this work, a kit that gives every new game a running start, and guidelines for turning annotated source code into pages people enjoy. Lemon64, C64.com and C64-Wiki already catalogue and celebrate the games, and we love and respect them. This site adds what’s underneath: how the games actually work.

Principles

The goal is human joy

Read and play, to immerse yourself in the game you treasure. Research and write, to share your excitement for the game you love.

Embrace and respect the C64 ecosystem

Link and boost, never scrape or duplicate. We wouldn’t be here without Lemon64 and the greats.

Anti-slop, pro-human

Joy means joy in reading, which means we care about writing. The writing on every minisite deserves as much craft as the reverse engineering. Agents can kickstart the content, but we take multiple passes to de-slop it and encourage a human voice.

Keeping the slop out

Plenty of readers, me included, stop reading at the first phrase that sounds machine-written, however good the analysis underneath. So the writing is held to rules as strict as the ones for the facts.

Ruled out by the house style

  • elegant
  • clever
  • fascinating
  • remarkable
  • ingenious
  • let’s dive in
  • here’s the thing
  • buckle up
  • put simply
  • it’s not X, it’s Y
  • more than just
  • The X Is A Y
Written last, to a house style

A game’s page is written in its own pass once the analysis is finished, never by the run that produced the facts. Then it is rewritten against the house style, paragraph by paragraph: no question answered in the next breath, no tidy triplets, no em-dash between every clause, no summary that repeats the section. The test is a person reading the page without noticing how it was made.

Every game page says who wrote it

Each game’s About tab labels its copy agent-draft until a person has read it, then agent, human-edited or human, and names the model and tools behind the analysis. Where a person and an agent disagree about the words, the person wins.

Tiers don’t round up

A Silver page is agent work, and says so under its title, with an invitation to edit it. Gold means a person has gone through every section, rewriting the clichés, cutting what’s dull, expanding what’s interesting and adding what the agent missed. An unattended run stays Silver, however well it reads.

Facts carry their evidence

Before a game reaches Silver, every fact on its pages has been traced in the code or watched happen in an emulator. Addresses link into the annotated source, so you can check a claim yourself. Where something isn’t known, the page says so.

What this site is not

It isn’t a ROM site, a general games wiki, an emulator project, or an AI slop farm.

  • No game files, ever. We never host or accept disk images, ROMs or emulator snapshots. You bring your own copy of a game, and every proposed change is checked automatically for files that must not be there.
  • Other people’s work stays theirs. Each game’s About tab links out to other places to love that game, and credits the people who made it where we know who they were.
  • Credit goes to people. A game’s contributors are the GitHub accounts in its history. Agents are listed as the tools they are, never as contributors.
  • Open source. Write-ups, facts and symbol maps are CC BY-SA 4.0 and the code is MIT. Every game page links to its files on GitHub, so anyone can correct it.

Write-ups we admire

A few favourites from elsewhere.

  • Bubble Bobble, by Victor Widell. Why level 1 usually gives you a shoe: the random numbers behind the power-ups. There are also posts on the wind currents and a fix for the continue bug.
  • The Fairlight Intro, by Ingo Hinterding. The 1987 intro, unpacked and disassembled.
  • Gridrunner: The Little Black Book, by mwenge. Jeff Minter’s Gridrunner read from its code, for anyone curious how 8-bit games worked under the hood.
  • Pitfall II and Spelunker, at Commodore 64 Brain. Both taken apart for hacks, such as a bigger jump for Pitfall Harry.
  • Endless, at adayinthelifeof.nl. The whole map of a 1985 Dutch maze game, all 1,024 by 512 characters of it, extracted with a debugger.
  • Rigel’s Revenge. Mastertronic’s 1987 text adventure, reconstructed from its binary, and playable in the browser.
  • GMC64. Garry Kitchen’s GameMaker, documented and rebuilt for the browser.

Questions

Is this going to disappear?
Nope. Everything is in a public repository under open licences, and the content is deliberately forkable, so the work doesn’t depend on any one person, me included. As soon as someone else has shipped a game, the project gets a second maintainer.
Is it only for the Commodore 64?
We’re proving it on the C64, but it’s designed to expand beyond that. The status page lists the other machines and what adding each one takes.
Aren’t you sucking the joy out of this by using agents?
We don’t require agents for the reverse engineering, but they’re by far the most efficient way to do it. Our first principle is human joy. If you want to reverse engineer by hand, go for it!
I’ve written about a game on my own site. Will you copy it?
No. We’ll link to it. Every game’s About tab has a list of other places to love that game: tell us about yours on Discord, or add it to the game’s game.json in a pull request.
