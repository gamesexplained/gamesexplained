Flowers of Crystal

Flowers of Crystal was still on sale on 24 October 2026, 42 years after it was written: 4Mation sold the BBC Micro original, made to run on Windows, as Retro Flowers of Crystal, on CD-ROM for £15.50, with the story book and the teacher's guide as PDFs.

Buy Retro Flowers of Crystal from 4Mation

A school adventure in BBC BASIC that runs itself again after every journey and keeps the player's progress in the 26 variables BASIC never clears. Its last picture, the Crystal Flower, is six pieces of machine code, one for each part of a dream the player collects.

01 · The game

The game

4Mation describes Flowers of Crystal as an adventure on the theme of ecology and conservation, in which the player helps young Jim save the planet Crystal from greedy Mr Grubble.

Super Jim at the Centre, where part 1 starts and where he brings the player back after every rescue.

The Face, who keeps the way into the Danger Area in part 2.

The story book that came with the game tells how Rumala, long ago, hid a bag of soil, a sack of fertiliser, a bottle of magic spell water and an enchanted gold pot, and hid the Crystal Flower in the place that became the Danger Area. Mr Grubble now has a bubble-gum factory and a gum forest on Crystal, and Grubble's Grabbers patrol it.

Part 1 is a journey between eight places on Crystal. At the Centre the player hears a weather report and chooses equipment: one of a warm coat, a raincoat and a drink; one of four testers; and two of a fright stick, a radio, bubble-gum and a disguise kit. Every journey needs a travel permit, issued by Jim, and meets a hazard on the way. Seven treasures are hidden in the other places, and bringing all seven back to the Centre ends part 1 with a hint: When you are asked your age you must say 197.

Part 2 is the Danger Area. The Face asks the player's age, and only 197 lets them in. The player has spells of four kinds, moving, vanishing, flower and lucky dip, to use against its dangers, and the flower spells give the six parts of a dream of the Crystal Flower. The castle's part, found with the other five, ends the game.

The disc's menu offers the story, the two parts of the adventure, and a utility that prints three worksheets for the classroom: Crystal Log, Crystal Notes and Grubble's Gum Forest Grid. Everything on it is BBC BASIC, eight programs in all, with fifteen pictures in machine code. Mike Matson wrote it a year after Granny's Garden.

02 · The Crystal Flower

Six pieces of machine code draw the Crystal Flower

Each part of the flower dream found in the Danger Area adds a piece to one picture. Each piece is a short routine of machine code that prints a table of teletext codes, and the six pieces together are the Crystal Flower.

The emulator's picture of the end of the game, with all six parts found.

Left, the picture as the page makes it: each routine's table, read from the listing, sent through the page's own copy of the operating system's screen output, then drawn with BBC.drawTeletext. Each routine run alone in jsbeeb, and the six together, left the same 1,000 bytes on the screen as the page does, and so did the whole picture at two moments of the game: Flower Dream 3, with the first and third parts held, and the end.

PROCfl draws the picture each time a part is found ($6090). For each part held, in the order F, L, O, W, E and R, it calls that part's routine: $0900, $0C00, $0AA3, $0995, $0C8E and $0A19. The variables that hold the parts, F%, L%, O%, W%, E% and R%, spell FLOWER in the order the picture draws them. The parts are numbered in another order, the order of the buttons: W%, from the Horror, is part 1, and F%, from the sixth of Rumala's Wisdom Stones, is part 6.

Every routine is the same eleven instructions. It stores 0 at &70 and &71, sends each byte of its table to OSWRCH, the operating system's routine that prints a character, and then branches over the table to the RTS after it. The branch's distance is the table's length, 127 bytes for F. The table holds what a PRINT statement would send: cursor moves (VDU 31 and two coordinates), colour codes and graphics characters. Nothing in either adventure reads &70 or &71.

Two pieces need BASIC's help. The F and E pieces each have a row or two of their own, printed from BASIC after the routine. W's table prints its rows 5 and 6 twice, and its first row 6 holds row 4's bytes, which the second row 6 covers.

The routines live at &0900-&0AFF and &0C00-&0CFF, below the program at &0E00, in pages the operating system keeps for the cassette and RS423 buffers and for redefined characters, which a Mode 7 game that loads nothing from tape does without. The sound program loads them there before it chains the adventure (the loader).

03 · The map

Super Jim's map scrolls inside a text window

Typing Map at any place in part 1 shows a Super Jim special, 42 rows tall, of which the screen shows 16. The arrow keys scroll it a row at a time, and the operating system does the scrolling.

The emulator's picture of the map as it opens, with the game's instructions under it.

The map as the page prints it from the DATA at $39DA, read from the listing, through the same copy of the screen output as the flower. Click the map and use the arrow keys, or the buttons. Opened, scrolled south with 28 presses of the up arrow (the last two at the end of the map) and north with 3 of the down arrow, the page's window matched jsbeeb's after every press, all 680 characters of it, on 32 screens.

Line 100 builds the map's rows as strings when the program starts, from the DATA at line 5140. Each row starts with a mark. A row marked 0 is one string. A row marked 1 is three strings and a count: a first string, the count's copies of a second, and a third. The second string is usually one character, a space or a solid block, so a long run of ground takes a few bytes; two rows repeat the six characters of a mountain. Twenty-eight of the 42 rows are stored that way.

PROCmap sets a text window over rows 0 to 16 with VDU 28 and prints the first 16 rows into it. The up arrow prints the next row on the window's bottom line, and the new line that ends the PRINT pushes the whole window up a row: the map moves north to show more of the south. The down arrow puts the cursor on the window's top line and moves it up one more (VDU 30, then 11), which pushes the window down, and prints the row above. The program never moves a character itself. Each row starts with code 157, new background, which makes the ground white from there to the right-hand edge.

04 · The journeys

Whether there is a path depends on the distance between two places

A journey follows a path, has none, falls in the river, or is sent somewhere else first. The game has no table of which it is: it works the answer out from where the two places are.

a paththe riversomewhere else firstno path

From each place down the side to each place along the top, as PROCp and FNl decide it ($2528, $6E2B), ported to the page and run on the positions in the listing. Every one of the 56 journeys was made in jsbeeb, and each went the way the table says.

Line 55 reads each place's position from the DATA at line 20000 ($6B96). FNl is the distance between two of them: the straight line, with the distance across counted at two thirds, rounded down to a whole number. The plot draws the places that way, so its distances are FNl's before the rounding. Choose two places, or click them on the plot.

PROCp then decides by the distance and by the places' numbers, Centre 0 to Mountains 7. It looks for the river first: a distance of 6 between places whose numbers add up to 9, or of 7 between places that add up to 10, falls in the river, and Super Jim fishes the player out, back at the Centre. Only Forest to Desert and Forest to Mountains do. A distance of 10 between places that add up to 4, the City and the Forest, sends the player to the Hills first, and a distance of 9, the Centre and the Forest, to the Factory first. After those, a distance of 3, 4 or 6 has a path, and so do a distance of 5 between places whose numbers add up to an even number and a distance of 10 between places that add up to 5. Anything else has no path: the game asks Are you sure? and sends two hazards instead of one.

The rules leave the Mountains with no path from anywhere, and the Desert with one, from the Lake. The Lake is 10 from the City and 10 from the Factory, and only the City gets a path, because the rule for 10 names the City and the Lake by their sum. River and Danger Area are refused as places, the second with It is FORBIDDEN. flashing.

05 · Weather and hazards

The weather report rules out two of the nine hazards

Every visit to the Centre gives a weather report, and the report decides which weather the next journeys can meet. The equipment offers one of a warm coat, a raincoat and a drink, and the report says which the weather can call for.

PROCw and PROChaz ($2B2E, $27FD) ported, with the report's words from line 20100 ($6D6F) and each hazard's first warning from the DATA from line 3020 ($2C10), read from the listing. The game's own PROChaz, run in jsbeeb 2,000 times for each of the three kinds of weather with a path and 2,000 without, chose only the hazards the page chooses, never one twice on a journey, and each about as often as the others.

PROCw picks one of six reports, two for each of sun, snow and rain, with RND(6), and sets one of three resident integers: M% for sun, H% for snow and R% for rain. PROChaz picks each hazard with RND(9) until it finds one not yet picked on this journey. Snow is thrown back unless H% is set, rain unless R% is and sun unless M% is, and a thrown-back hazard counts as picked. So each hazard of a journey is one of seven, with equal chances.

HazardThe two choicesWhat decides it

The hazards of lines 3020-3100. The choices are the game's words; the outcomes are from the code that follows each line of DATA.

Most hazards are decided by the equipment, and the rest by RND(4): one time in four the snow loses the player, the sun swells their tongue, the Blids capture them, or the Fright Freak ends them. Three outcomes tell the player that some of their belongings are gone, and take every one (all of the belongings).

06 · Fibber of the Lake

The lie detector reads Fibber's own table

At the Lake, Fibber gives six instructions, and the player does the opposite of each one that is a lie. The lie detector from the City's yellow building answers TRUTH? for each.

FNLake's questions ($51CF, $52DE, $53FB) ported, with the five instructions, their choices and which are lies read from the DATA at $56BE. In jsbeeb the detector answered NO to the cave, the steps and the mist and YES to the fence and the hill, and answering by it beat Fibber.

The first instruction is always You will have to leave my lake., and Stay is right. The other five come from line 12280 in an order picked at random, each with a 1 for a lie or a 2 for the truth. For a lie the second choice is right, and for the truth the first. With the detector, TRUTH? shows NO or YES from the same number. A wrong choice gets Caught you out! That was a fib! and the visit ends. All five right gets Clever you! You've beaten me., and the special water, if the player brought the water tester.

Before the questions, Fibber says that three of his six instructions will be the opposite of what they should be. Four of them are lies (Fibber's warning).

07 · Starting again after every journey

Every journey ends with RUN, which clears everything but A% to Z%

The adventures never return from a journey. Each journey of part 1 and each visit of part 2 ends with RUN, which starts the program again and clears every variable but the 26 resident integers, A% to Z%. Part 2 keeps its twenty spells in two of them.

Click a spell to change it: never had, held (purple), or used (struck through). PROCspst and PROCspre ($54AD, $5501, in part 2) ported; the names of the rows are line 28200's. In jsbeeb, spells 1, 2 and 3 held gave S% = 7, and after the river took spell 3 S% was 3 and T% 4. Spells 1, 2, 3, 6 and 7 held gave 103.

RUN also empties BASIC's stacks, so the procedures, loops and GOSUBs that made the journey are gone with it. Each place's routine is called by name, EVAL("FN"+l$(N%)) in part 1 and EVAL("FNL"+STR$(Z%)) in part 2, and its return goes to a line that runs the program again. A fresh start is the same RUN with A% set to 0, and so is any error, through ON ERROR A%=0:RUN.

Part 1 keeps the treasures in single integers: I% the lie detector, K% the goggles, C% the city pass, G% the gold pot, W% the special water, F% the fertiliser and B% the bag of soil. N% is the place the journey goes to, and P% becomes it after the RUN. The equipment is in none of them. It is in DIM e% 11, a block of twelve bytes, and BASIC puts that block at the same address after each RUN and never clears it, so the bytes are still there.

Part 2 has twenty spells, each never had, held or used, and too few integers left for one each. PROCspst packs them before every RUN: bit p−1 of S% for each spell p held, and of T% for each spell used. PROCspre unpacks them at the start of every visit, from bit 19 down. The five spells a game begins with, 4, 8, 12, 16 and 20, are S% = 559,240.

08 · The loader

The adventure moves itself and types its own commands

An adventure arrives at &1900, above the disc system's workspace, and runs at &0E00, 2,816 bytes lower. It copies itself down, then finishes the job by pressing a function key that the program before it defined.

  1. The sound program, N1 for part 1, defines function key f0 and chains AD1, which BASIC loads at &1900.
  2. Line 1 sees that PAGE, where the program starts, is not &0E00, and goes to line 32220.
  3. Lines 32220 and 32225 select the cassette filing system, so the disc system's workspace is free, copy the program to &0E00 four bytes at a time, lower TOP by 11 pages, set PAGE to &0E00 and RUN the copy.
  4. At &0E00 line 1's test is false. Line 2 writes REM's token over it, so the test is never made again.
  5. Line 5 shrinks the text window to one character in the corner and puts the code for f0 in the keyboard buffer. Line 6 is STOP, and BASIC waits at its prompt.
  6. The prompt reads f0, and the operating system types f0's commands, unseen in the one-character window.
  7. The game runs at &0E00, without its loader.

f0's commands are read from line 3 of N1, which loads at &1900; the lines they delete are part 1's lines 1-6, 32220 and 32225 ($1900, $706C), whose lengths come from the listing. In jsbeeb both adventures moved to &0E00 and ran there.

The commands are DELETE1,6, 32220, 32225, MODE7 and RUN, each followed by RETURN. Typed at the prompt, a line number on its own deletes that line, so the first three commands remove the loader and the copyright lines, and the last two clear the screen and start the game. The key's definition lives in the operating system's buffer at &0B00, which CHAIN leaves alone, so it survives from the sound program into the adventure. The copy that runs is 206 bytes smaller than the file on the disc.

The copyright lines, 3 and 4, also guard the program on the disc. Each is a REM that holds bell characters, a green colour code and the copyright, and line 4 ends with code 21, which turns the screen off. A LIST of the program rings the bell, shows the copyright in green, and then shows nothing (LIST).
