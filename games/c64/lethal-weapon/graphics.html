1992 · Ocean Software

Graphics

Every sprite shape that is in memory on stage 1A, drawn from the listing's bytes, and how the scroller and the sprite multiplexer put them on screen.

01 · The things that move

Riggs and the men on the docks

The player walks, climbs and shoots from scripts of frame numbers; the enemies step through a table that the stage's overlay brings with it. Both play below.

The player's scripts and stage 1A's enemies, animating. The player's walks, climbs and poses come from the scripts at $24AA and $2542, one entry each pass of the main loop. The enemies step through the stage's animation table at $387B, where the byte for each frame is what to add to reach the next. Their steps are shown here at one every eighth pass, because the rate depends on each behaviour's own counters. Every sprite is multicolour: black ($D025 = 0) and light red ($D026 = $0A, set at $13CD) are shared, and each object brings its own third colour.

Riggs is drawn in blue (colour 6) and Murtaugh in brown (colour 9), the value the game keeps in $BF. When Murtaugh is chosen, the stage loader reads SP0Z in place of SP00, so the frames shown here are Riggs's. The enemies' colours come from their records at $3970: the gunmen of behaviour $26 are red or brown, the thrower of $2E yellow, the gunman of $28 purple, and the hiding man of $30 dark grey.

02 · Every frame

Seven sprite files

A stage loads its sprites as files, listed in its overlay at $383C. These are stage 1A's, frame by frame, in the colour of the object that uses each file.

Frames $4E to $F8. Frame $4E is blank: the stage loader clears it, and the player flickers to it after a hit. SP00's later frames ($8F to $A3) also hold hearts, guns, and other shapes that are not the player; they are drawn in his colour here, though the object that shows each one brings its own. Frame $B4, at $6D00, is not in the listing and is left out. SP90, the boss's file, loads first and over the start of the level map. On the last stage the loader clears frames $AD, $AF, $B1 and $B3 ($1D3A), so those enemies are invisible there. Other stages load other files that only the disk holds, so their shapes are not on this page.

03 · The screen

A fifth of the screen each frame

A level is a map of 288 by 70 characters, built when the stage loads. Two screens take turns: one is shown while the other is redrawn one character over.

When the player nears an edge, $2570 picks one of eight directions. Each frame the scroll moves the fine-scroll registers one pixel and calls one routine from a table of 128 ($178F, by direction, screen and step). Most of these copy four or five rows of the shown screen into the hidden one, shifted by a character. When the fine scroll wraps, the screens swap ($1579), colour RAM is shifted to match ($2B3F and its seven siblings) and the new edge is drawn from the level map ($1593). Every object on the level moves the other way by the same amount.

The map itself is built by $0673 from 2×2-character tiles: 144 tile columns of 35 tiles. The tiles come from the stage's .BLK file and the tile map from its .MAP file, which holds 64 rows. Stage 1A uses rows 29 to 63 of it; stage 1AX, a door away, uses rows 0 to 34 of the same map. The Maps tab draws every stage.

04 · Sixteen objects

One table, eight sprites

Everything that moves is one of sixteen objects in a table in zero page. Before each frame they are sorted by line, and the raster interrupt hands them to the eight sprites from the top of the screen down.

ByteHolds
$AF+ntype: 0 a walking thug, 2 to $0A pickups, $12 to $22 hazards, $24 and up an enemy behaviour, $FF the player, his bullets and empty slots
$BF+ncolour, with the ninth bit of x
$CF+nsprite frame
$DF+nline on screen, 0 for an empty slot
$EF+nx

Object 0 is the player and objects 1 and 2 his bullets. The sort ($F2B3) is an insertion sort, cheap because the order rarely changes. The interrupt at $E1AC places one object per sprite until the next one is too far down, then sets itself for 13 lines above it and continues there. Each behaviour is a routine named in a table ($077D) and called by rewriting the operand of one JSR ($0FA6).
