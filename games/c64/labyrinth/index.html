Commodore 64 · 1986 · Lucasfilm Games

Labyrinth

A film tie-in that starts as a typed adventure on a city street and ends up in a maze you walk with a joystick. The popcorn costs two dollars when the menu says $1.25, and the guards in the maze answer differently depending on how many doors you have opened.

01 · The adventure

Thirteen hours to reach Jareth

The game opens on a city block outside a movie theater. You name your character, answer the setup questions, and explore the streets in text, with two word wheels that hold the verbs and the objects.2 The ticket counter will not let you in without a ticket, which costs five dollars ($90F4, $9327). The popcorn stand has a trick of its own: the menu asks $1.25, but the woman takes two dollars and leaves two quarters in change ($93E1). Walking into the cinema is the way into the maze, and the text adventure follows each step.

Inside the maze the story is a race. The Player’s Guide sets the goal: reach Jareth before thirteen hours expire.1 The clock counts in whole hours, and some actions spend them: at the Wall of Hands, the eighth manicure takes one hour, and a game with no hours left ends ($0C61). You steer the character with joystick port 1 along the walls of each room, and the same wheels give the commands. Controls covers both.

The maze begins at the entrance gate, which leads to the first corridor. Its exits open into the Brick Hallway, a row of doors where your position decides which you can open. Beyond it, two guards claim that one of them always lies and the other tells the truth, and they will not say more until you have opened every door in the hallway. A random bit set when their room loads changes what the two doorways do; in the recorded route, the right doorway ends in a fall. The Wall of Hands follows, where the manicure command needs shears, and then the Stone Corridor with its door, arch, and ladder.

02 · The release

Lucasfilm Games’ adventure

Lucasfilm Games made Labyrinth, based on Jim Henson’s film and published by Activision in 1986. The disk’s credits name David Fox as production manager and programmer, Charlie Kellner as technical director and lead programmer, and Gary Winnick for character animation and background art. David M. Martin Jr. is credited with the music and Commodore sound.1

This explanation follows the two-sided English Commodore 64 disk edition, which keeps three game slots. The About page names the copy studied and the sources.
