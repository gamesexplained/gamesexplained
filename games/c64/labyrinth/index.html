Commodore 64 · 1986 · Lucasfilm Games

Labyrinth

A trip to the cinema becomes a thirteen-hour escape from the Goblin King. Lucasfilm’s adaptation of Jim Henson’s film begins in words, then pulls you into a graphical maze: a transition suggested by The Hitchhiker’s Guide to the Galaxy author Douglas Adams.5

01 · The game

Through the cinema screen

You begin on a rainy city street. Before there are goblins to avoid, there is a cinema ticket to buy and a popcorn seller to deal with. You choose your character’s name, sex, and favourite colour, then explore through descriptions of the places around you.

The wheels remain when the pictures arrive. Once the film draws you into the maze, you walk your character with the joystick and combine words to speak to its inhabitants or use the things you find. Commands such as ask, congratulate, and manicure leave room for curiosity alongside the work of finding a way through.

Jareth, the Goblin King, gives you thirteen hours to reach him and overcome his power. The journey takes you through corridors and strange encounters drawn from the film, including the Wall of Hands and the Bog of Eternal Stench. The Player’s Guide presents Jareth himself as your mocking host, promising imprisonment and offering suspicious reassurance.1

02 · The opening

Douglas Adams and the opening

Programmer and production manager David Fox recalled a week of design discussions in London with Adams and a group that included writer Christopher Cerf, known for his work on Sesame Street, and Activision’s Brenda Laurel.

Fox credited Adams with the change from text to graphics. The comparison was The Wizard of Oz: an ordinary world presented in black and white gives way to colour when Dorothy reaches Oz. In Labyrinth, entering the film changes how the game presents its world. The player crosses the cinema screen at the same moment the adventure becomes a place to walk around.5

The word wheels had a practical origin too. In a later interview, Fox said the team lacked time to build a full system for interpreting typed sentences before the film’s release. Their “slot machine interface” displayed the available verbs and objects instead. A player could compose a command from the supplied words.6

03 · The makers

The people who made the maze

The printed credits give Charlie Kellner the roles of technical director and lead programmer. Fox and Kevin Furry also programmed the game.

Its characters and scenery were shared work. Gary Winnick contributed character animation and background art, Ken Macklin worked on character animation, and James St. Louis supplied background art. David M. Martin Jr. is credited with the music and Commodore sound.

Laurel produced the Commodore edition for Activision and contributed to the design. The guide also names Adams, Cerf, Noah Falstein, and Stephen Arnold as design contributors. These are the credits in the disk edition’s Player’s Guide, which accompanies the version examined here.1

04 · Reception

Walking, waiting, and solving

The March 1987 Zzap!64 review captures both the attraction and the frustration of this approach. Paul Sumner praised the smooth character animation but thought the pace too slow for action players and the puzzles too slight for adventure enthusiasts. Julian Rignall similarly objected to the time spent walking.

Gary Penn’s response was warmer. He described becoming absorbed enough to play almost to completion in one long sitting, before getting bored outside the Goblin Castle. He admired the large animated characters but complained that loading interrupted play even on disk.

The magazine’s overall verdict was 50%. Its comments explain more than the number: Labyrinth could sustain an evening of exploration and amusing encounters, while its walking speed and repeated loads tested the reviewers’ patience. The review listed the British cassette at £9.99 and disk at £14.99.7

05 · Looking inside

The city follows you into the maze

One small decision at the popcorn stand survives the change of worlds. The seller drops a nickel, which you can pocket or return. The game remembers.

If you keep the nickel, a teenager in the cinema takes your whole box when offered popcorn. Otherwise, he takes a handful. Later, the Wise Man’s payment routines also check that earlier choice: keeping the nickel can mean receiving less change. The nickel demonstration shows those consequences.

Other jokes become puzzles through repetition. At the Wall of Hands, giving manicures advances a sequence of replies. The eighth accepted manicure spends an hour and interrupts the scene with a card announcing the lost time.
