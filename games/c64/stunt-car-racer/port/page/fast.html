The Fast CPU switch beside the screen races as a C64 with a much faster processor would. The race's main loop goes round once every video frame, 50 times a second, and each pass draws a new view into the game's own bitmaps, which its interrupt puts on show. On a C64 the same loop manages about seven a second.

A faster processor alone would make a faster game, since each pass moves the cars a fixed step. Fast CPU keeps the game's speed instead, and the game says what that is: its clocks add 0.14 seconds for each pass (TICK_CLOCK, $109C), seven frames' worth. So seven of Fast CPU's passes stand for one of the C64's, and each takes a seventh of every step: of the car's move and its turn, of the springs' and the engine's push, of the crane's swing, of the other car's move along the track and across it, and of each speck of dust thrown up at the road's edge. The sevenths go through the game's own code and add up to the C64's step.

The C64 falls behind its seven frames. It takes about 7.25 over most passes on Little Ramp, and 10.5 while the crane lowers the car, so everything slows with it and its clocks lose time: from the start line to the jump, they count 14.7 seconds in 15.9 seconds of real time. A faster processor would not fall behind, so Fast CPU keeps to the seven frames there. Where the C64 is quicker than seven, as the car falls off the track and there is little to draw, Fast CPU follows it: the page knows what each part of a pass costs on a C64, so before each of the C64's passes it works out the frames the C64 would take over the work of the last one, and shares that pass among as many of its own.

What the game does once a pass is done once for every seven: its clocks and the laps, the count of passes, the engine's note's aim (the interrupt still moves the note on each video frame, as on a C64), the countdowns, the damage's hold on a broken part, the other car's dice for a swerve or a bounce, and each sound it starts. A spring's press is measured against where its wheel was a C64's pass ago, as the game measures it.

Landings differ. On a C64 the car falls a whole pass's step into the road before a spring pushes back, so how hard a landing presses the springs depends on where in a pass the wheels reach the road. With Fast CPU they meet it within a video frame. In 16 practice races on Little Ramp from the same start, each with a short turn at a different moment, a spring's hardest press was 7 to 15 on a C64 and 9 to 12 with Fast CPU. The damage at the car's three points came to 6.8 on average with Fast CPU against 7.8 on a C64: more in 10 of the races, less in 5. So with Fast CPU the hardest landings do less damage and the softest a little more.

Some things change with the finer steps. The stick is read on every pass, so a short tap steers for as long as it is held, where a C64 holds it for the whole of a pass. The frames come at the C64's 50.12 a second: a display at 50 or 100 Hz shows each for the same time, and at 60 Hz one frame in five stays up for two refreshes. The menus, the panels before and after a race, and the restart after a wreck run at the C64's own speed. Switched off, the game is the C64's again from where it stands.
