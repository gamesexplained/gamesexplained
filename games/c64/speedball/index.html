Speedball

The Bitmap Brothers' armoured ball game, converted for the Commodore 64 by Pantheon Software. Its seven tunes play here on the game's own sound driver, and its knockout cup, league and tackles hide bugs that change who you are really playing.

01 · The game

Five a side in steel armour

Two teams of five, a keeper and four outfield players each, fight over a steel ball in a walled arena. There are no fouls.

You steer whichever of your outfield players is nearest the ball, and the rest of the team runs itself. Fire throws the ball, or, without it, jumps or slides into a tackle. The pitch scrolls up and down; the ball goes into a goal only along the ground, and a warp tunnel in each side wall sends it out of the other wall. Tokens and power-up tiles appear on the floor, and after each match the tokens buy bribes and better figures in a shop.

The menu offers a knockout cup of ten rounds, a league of eleven teams, and a league for two players. You choose one of three teams, Verna, Lacata or Draco, each with its own stamina, power and skill.

02 · The music

Music: a goal plays the scoring side's own tune

The menu's credits name Dave Whittaker for the music and the sound effects. The driver plays seven tunes, from the menu's theme to the jingles for a goal.

The game's own sound driver, run on the site's 6502 from the bytes in the Source tab, through the site's model of the SID chip. Each tune starts as the game starts it (tune_play, $8B2F) and plays one call a frame, as the game's interrupt makes it (music_frame, $8B5F). A test holds the page to the driver, every write of every frame.

The driver is FILE0, loaded at $0810. Each tune has seven bytes in tune_table ($131E): its tempo, then where each of the three voices finds its list of patterns. Once a frame the driver adds the tempo to a running total (music_frame_voices, $094E), and each time the total carries past 255 every voice steps through its pattern. Tunes 5 and 6, the goal jingles, end on their own; the others loop.

03 · One frame

One frame, rebuilt from memory

The match screen is a scrolling pitch of characters with the panel split off below it, and the players are sprites reused down the screen.

Drawn by the site's C64 library from one frame recorded in the emulator: the video chip's registers at the top of the frame, every change the game's raster interrupts make during it, and the memory the chip reads. All 104,448 pixels match the emulator's own picture of the same frame, for both screens.

The game runs one interrupt handler (irq_handler, $86D3) and steps it through a chain of slots, each a raster line, the video registers to set there and a routine (irq_slot_run, $8704). In a match the slots place the sprites: ten players, the ball and the four parts of the ball launcher are sorted by height every pass (sprites_sort, $8ADD) and handed out to the eight sprites from the top of the screen down. The pitch is a map of 40 columns by 74 rows at $F300, built before each match (pitch_map_build, $917D) and copied into whichever of two screens is hidden when the camera moves a row (scroll_update, $9292).

04 · The pitch

The bounce domes move after every match

Four domes stand on the pitch, and their places change after every match, through ten layouts in turn.

The arena in the game's own coordinates, from the bounds the code tests: the side walls at x $1C and $108, the goals between x $64 and $C7, the warp tunnels between y $108 and $127. The domes come from the layout tables dome_x ($2E73) and dome_y ($2E9B, $2EC3), four entries a layout, each the corner of a dome four characters square.

The layout number $1ED0 moves on after every match, from 0 to 9 and round again (match_over, $3320), and domes_place ($9241) turns its four entries into places in the map. A dome turns the ball only while it rolls along the ground (dome_bounce), and the players run straight through it. The ball launcher stands in the middle, where no tile is ever placed (tile_place, $A94C).

05 · Tiles

Tokens come up more often than all the power-ups together

A tile appears on the floor 50 passes after the last one was taken. Your controlled player takes it by standing on it.

The game's own tile_choose ($A994) run on the site's 6502 from the listing, a thousand times a press, with the game type set for one player or two. Each bar is a tile type the routine chose.

tile_choose draws a random number from 0 to 19, or to 20 in a two player game, and takes ten from it, counting anything below 0 as 0. The result picks an entry in the table at $BB21, 00 01 02 03 04 05 06 08 08 08 0A. Entry 0 is a token, so eleven numbers in twenty give a token, and W, the ball that tackles whoever it hits, three times as often as any other power-up. J, which reverses the other player's stick, is the twenty-first number and so comes only in a two player game. The manual's electron bolt E (type 7) and eight-way burst M (type 9) are not in the table; their entries in the effect table at $BBBF point at W's routine (tile_ball_tackles, $9A9F).

A power-up lasts 120 passes, ten more for each bribe of the timer, with a countdown of ten stages in the panel (tile_countdown, $9AB3). The computer's team picks up tiles too, but only a token does anything for it (tile_check, $99FA).

06 · Teams

The computer's ten teams grow stronger in order

Your three teams have figures of their own; the ten others are built from templates that grow stronger in order.

Read from the listing: the three human teams at $B9D6, $B9D9 and $B9DC, the templates at $B9DF-$BA2B, the names at team_names ($303C). Skill is what the cards show, 255 less the tackle figure the code keeps. The delays are the computer's, in passes of play.

A new league or knockout copies the templates into eleven team records (team_record_init, $3F32), and every match loads the computer's side from one of them (opponent_load, $3F81). Lower delays make the computer aim, throw and save sooner, so template 10, Persus, is the strongest. The league keeps the records in template order. The knockout shuffles them, and then loads the wrong one: see Discoveries.

A league awards 100 points for a win, 20 for a draw and 5 for every goal (league_record, $994A). The other four matches of each week are settled with random numbers, weighted by the two teams' tackle figures (league_week_end, $8519). The knockout gives 2 points for a won leg and 1 for a draw, and 3 points take you through to the next round (knockout_round_end, $8195).

07 · Tackles

How a tackle is decided

Two players who touch are measured against each other by the way they face, unless one of them is sliding.

Every pass the game measures the distance between each of the 45 pairs of players (pair_distances, $B038) and then looks for pairs that touch (tackles_check, $8DD6). For two opponents that touch, tackle_resolve ($8EF5) decides. A sliding player brings the other down when a random number from 0 to 255 is at least the victim's tackle figure, unless the victim's side has power-up P. Otherwise the two players' facings, looked up in $B996, say who wins, and a player you control wins only while you hold fire. The loser falls and loses stamina by the winner's power (stamina_drain, $908A); the winner takes the ball if the loser had it.

Below 12 stamina a player runs a step slower (player_dir_step, $9F98), and below 6 a player's throws are a step weaker (player_move, $9E45). Both bugs in this part of the game, a table looked up thirteen bytes early and a skill figure that works backwards, are on the Discoveries tab.

08 · The shop

What the tokens buy

After every match the shop sells eleven items for the tokens you collected.

Prices from shop_cost ($2C23); what each does from its routine, through the table at shop_effect_lo ($BB83).

In a one player game the four items that weaken the computer are kept until the next match loads its team, and then cleared (attributes_handicap, $AEF5): each match, every leg of a knockout round and extra time included, loads the computer's figures afresh, so they last one match. The computer collects tokens as you do and never spends them.

09 · Copy protection

Two timers that must keep in step

The game checks that two timers in the computer's second interface chip are running, and running together, in every frame of a match.

At start-up the game sets timers A and B of CIA 2 (the C64's second interface chip) to the same period, $231F cycles, and starts them four cycles apart (game_start, $201A). It never uses them for timing. Three checks read them instead:

The interrupt handler reads timer B's control register on every interrupt, through an undocumented instruction, and when the timer has stopped it sets the match clock's target to 255, so the match never ends (irq_handler, $86D3). The keyboard reader makes the same test and sends the interrupt chain to a slot that does not exist (keys_read, $8891). At the top of the match screen, irq_slot_play_top ($8745) reads timer A and then timer B four cycles later; started four cycles apart, the two read the same, and their EOR, added to $2DD0, stays 0. That byte is ORed into the joystick, used as the frame-wait flag and picks the league's save file, so on a copy whose timers drift the stick jams and the game hangs. The four reads go through a different mirror of the timer registers each frame (irq_slot_shop_row, $881D), which hides them from a search for the registers' own addresses.

10 · Sound effects

Sound: over half the driver's effects are never played

The driver holds 26 sound effects and the launcher's hum. A match uses twelve of the effects.

The game's own driver again, each effect started as the game starts it (sound_play, $8B80) after the kick-off's launcher has set the volume. The ones marked unused are records in effect_table ($0F16) that no call in the game asks for.

An effect is a record of 20 bytes: its length in frames, two starting frequencies, envelopes, waveforms and slides for voices 1 and 3. Starting one takes those voices from the music (effect_start, $0E7A). The launcher's hum is voice 2 alone (hum_start, $1155), held while the launcher spins and stopped when it shoots the ball out (launcher_sequence, $3739).
