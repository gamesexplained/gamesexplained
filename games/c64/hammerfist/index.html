1990 · Activision · Commodore 64

Hammerfist

Two bodies keep separate energy. Their rooms, sprite pieces, and music banks reveal how the escape works.

01 · game

Two bodies share one escape

Hammerfist and Metalisis are human minds trapped in holographic bodies. They fight through the Master's installations, break security systems, and open routes to the next area. Hammerfist supplies punches and powered fists. Metalisis supplies kicks and acrobatics.

You can change bodies during play. Each body keeps its own energy, so a change can save a damaged character while the other recovers. Charging a body too far causes an explosion.

02 · music

Music: every stored selection

The game reads four sets of code and graphics from disk. The first three sets share a music bank. The fourth replaces that bank, and the loading portrait has another. The buttons expose every selection stored in those three players, including the loading bank's other selections.

The game's own music routines run on a model of the 6502, the C64's main processor. They feed a model of its SID sound chip. All 12 selections were run for 600 calls, with the emitted register writes checked. The model offers both SID filter variants. Main player $EEAE; fourth-load player $F16A. The loading player comes from the loading image at $EC4F.

03 · effects

Sound: eighteen effect definitions

The sound-effect player can assign an effect to one of three voices, the chip’s independent sound channels. A priority table decides whether a new request may replace the voice's existing effect. These buttons use the game's allocator, including its initial sound mode.

All 18 definitions were run for 600 calls. Definition 17 produces no gated sound in this isolated setup. The shared allocator is $32DA; effect records start at $FEDA. Low notes can be difficult to hear on laptop speakers.

04 · energy

An extra charge can destroy a body

A recharge step adds two energy units to the active character. A result below 225 is kept. A result of 225 or more starts the explosion, empties that body's energy, and switches characters. This makes 224 a dangerous amount of energy to bring to a recharge point.

One recharge update, held against native VICE instruction traces at $9159. Fourth-load room 7 caps energy at 224 and switches bodies without the explosion; see $913C and $9162. This isolates the recharge rule; it does not play a route to a recharge point.

05 · screen

The screen changes partway down

The status panel and the room use different sets of eight-by-eight-pixel character pictures. Interrupts, short routines triggered at chosen screen lines, change the video settings as the display is drawn. Other interrupts reuse the chip’s movable pictures, called sprites, lower down the screen.

This paused first-room presentation is rebuilt from video memory. The recording also supplies each change to the chip’s registers, the numbers that control its display. It matches all 104,448 pixels of the VICE reference image. Turning the changes off shows why one set of video registers cannot describe the whole frame. The hardware interrupt entry is $196A; the game clock advances at $6743 while pause is clear.

06 · controls

The controls remember short sequences

The joystick's direction and fire button select more than movement. The game remembers its action step and recent fire presses. A sequence of presses can therefore mean a doorway move or a weapon change.

07 · world

Rooms are made from reusable tiles

A room holds ten columns of five tiles. Each tile expands to a square of sixteen character cells. A separate bit can reflect a tile, so the same artwork faces either way. Room tables choose colours and a character bank before the game draws the background.

Objects and security systems sit above that background. The game remembers which systems have been destroyed. Those saved states select replacement tiles and change the invisible lines used to stop the player at walls and floors.

08 · cast

A moving body is assembled from pieces

The player is drawn with two overlapping sprite images. Some poses add a weapon image. Enemies and drops use a second format: a list of sprite pieces with positions and control bits. Animation tables choose the next shape and how many game-clock ticks to hold it. One tick is one PAL display frame while play is unpaused.
