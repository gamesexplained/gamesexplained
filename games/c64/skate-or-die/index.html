Commodore 64 · 1987 · Electronic Arts

Skate or Die!

A skate shop run by a purple-haired loudmouth connects five events, from half-pipe tricks to fighting on boards. Behind Skate or Die! were developers who had worked at Epyx and a British composer whose sampled guitars helped lead him to a job in California.

01 · The game

Start at Rodney’s

Rodney leans across his shop counter with a purple mohawk, a tattoo, and advice that changes with whatever you point at. Hover over practice and he tells you to quit talking and get skating. Up to eight players can sign in for competition and choose their board colours, or head straight out to practise.

Outside, you steer through a small town square and leave along the road to your chosen event. Each destination asks something different of the board.

Freestyle gives you ten passes through a half-pipe to perform tricks and land them safely. High Jump uses the ramp for a simpler contest: build speed and record the greatest height you can. Downhill Race sends you through a park against the clock, negotiating obstacles and fitting in stunts.

The other events put an opponent within striking distance. Downhill Jam is an alley race where punches and kicks can interrupt somebody else’s run. Pool Joust sends two skaters into an empty swimming pool, with one carrying a padded pole to knock the other off.1

02 · The cast

Rodney’s dear boy

A lone player still has somebody to fight. Rodney introduces his son Lester, the computer-controlled rival in Downhill Jam and the toughest of the Pool Joust opponents. The pool also offers Poseur Pete and Aggro’ Eddie, giving you opponents to learn before taking on Lester.

Their names are backed by different behaviour. Pete turns at random; Eddie watches where you are on the pool wall before dropping in. Lester considers your speed and height when timing his tricks. Those differences make the choice of opponent part of learning the event.

03 · The makers

From Epyx to Electronic Arts

The title screen names Michael Kosaka, Stephen Landrum, and David Bunch. The Commodore 64 credits transcribed by MobyGames identify Kosaka with the graphics and Landrum and Bunch with programming.6 In James Hague’s 1993 interview, Landrum describes himself as the game’s lead programmer.2

Landrum joined Electronic Arts in February 1987 after working at Epyx.2 Merman’s retrospective in The Retrogaming Times also places Kosaka among the former Epyx developers who moved to EA to make a skateboarding compilation. Epyx’s Summer Games and Winter Games had already shown the appeal of gathering separate sporting events into a competition. Skate or Die! gave that format a shopkeeper who talked back and rivals who could hit back.5

04 · The music

The guitars that led to California

For title composer Rob Hubbard, Skate or Die! was part of a move across the Atlantic. He recalled meeting EA’s Mark Lewis at an awards event in London and accepting an invitation to work in America for a couple of months. During that visit he made the C64 music, mixing sampled guitars with sounds generated by the computer’s SID sound chip. EA then offered him a permanent job in California.4

“You might say that piece of NMI code changed my life!” Hubbard told Arcade Attack. The code he meant was an interrupt routine: a small piece of programming that repeatedly interrupts other work to feed recorded sound to the chip.4

The title uses seven short recordings, played at different pitches and mixed with the chip’s synthesised voices. Listen to the title music and its samples.

05 · The reception

Praise with a wait between events

Zzap!64 awarded Skate or Die! a 92% Sizzler. Its verdict, reproduced in Merman’s retrospective, praised the sports simulation but singled out the “annoyingly slow loading system” as the reason it fell short of a higher award. Merman also quotes ACE praising the graphics and animation while finding the competition straightforward and enjoyable.5

Loading was part of moving between these different games. Each event brings its own program into memory, and the two-sided disk can require a flip before play continues. The variety that sends you from a half-pipe to an alley also means waiting for the next destination.

06 · Inside the game

Looking inside

The code explains some rules that are easy to misread while playing. High Jump keeps the height of the pass that ends your attempt. Reach twelve feet early and finish with an eight-foot pass, and eight feet is your result.

The music has a quieter surprise. High Jump chooses its next musical phrase using a random-number routine, but every fresh load begins with the same saved starting value. Its first performance therefore follows the same sequence; later attempts can take another route through the phrases.
