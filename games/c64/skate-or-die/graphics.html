Commodore 64 · 1987 · Electronic Arts

Skate or Die!

How each screen of the game is drawn, from the bytes the events load: the half-pipe that High Jump and the Freestyle ramp share, the shop and the town, the two downhill courses, and the pool. What happens on each is on the Levels page.

The picture

One bitmap, with a hires strip on top

The half-pipe, the stands, and the sky are a single multicolour bitmap. The bitmap arrives packed in its own file and is unpacked once, when the event starts ($1B6A).

Drawn in the page from the bytes of the listing: the bitmap at $4000, two colours per cell from the screen matrix at $6400, the third from the copy of colour RAM at $6000, on a black background ($089E). The top text row is drawn hires, as the game shows it. The skater, the ruler's black marks, and the shadow are sprites and are not part of the picture. The status line's words are written into the bitmap when the event starts; the listing holds them as they were in the snapshot.

The picture is stored in file $10 with a simple run-length code: a byte from $00 to $7F is followed by that many plus one bytes to copy, and a byte from $80 up repeats the next byte that many minus 127 times ($1B6A). The unpacker writes into the space where the poses will go, and a copy loop then moves 8,000 bytes of pixels to the bitmap, 1,000 colour pairs to the screen matrix and 1,000 colours to a spare kilobyte at $6000 ($08F2). That spare copy is what goes into the C64's colour RAM each time the event starts ($0A50), because colour RAM sits under the input and output chips and cannot be loaded from disk directly.

Two raster interrupts split the screen each frame. The first, at line 249, switches multicolour off, so the top text row is drawn hires ($0B21): that row holds the status line, PRACTICE, PASS, and HEIGHT in a font of 64 characters, drawn inverted into the bitmap ($126D). The second, at line 55, waits three lines and switches multicolour back on for the picture below ($0CA3).

The scenery

A crowd that cheers harder for higher jumps

Sixty-nine squares of the picture, each eight pixels by eight, have a second frame. The first eight are the four flags on the poles, and the other 61 are heads and arms in the stands. To swap one, animate_cell ($192E) copies the other frame's eight bytes of pixels into the bitmap and its colours into the screen matrix and colour RAM, and flips a bit that remembers which frame is showing ($1CA5).

The flags move on their own: about one frame in eight, one flag changes ($199B). The crowd depends on the skater. Each time the skater turns at the top of the right wall, half the height of that pass becomes the crowd's level ($1AD0), and on each frame one random head moves if a random number falls under that level ($19AE). The chance is the level out of 256: after a five-foot pass a head moves on about one frame in thirteen, after a twelve-foot pass on about one frame in five.

The skater

Five sprites, 158 poses

Every pose the skater can strike is a record of 30 bytes: five parts, one for each of the hardware sprites 0 to 4 ($095D). Press a pose to see it larger.

Colour set

All 158 records at $7000, drawn facing right, as on the right half of the ramp. Each part names a shape in the 16 KB of shapes from $8284, its size, and where it sits. Sprites 1 and 2 are multicolour and take white and dark blue as their shared colours; sprites 0, 3, and 4 are single-colour. The colour sets are the two at $2D7F and $2D87; the game picks one at random for each attempt ($19D3).

Play

The pose sequences High Jump steps through, read from its tables at $2A35-$2AB6 and played in a loop at four poses a second, a pace chosen for the page; the game moves to the next pose when its step counter $32 advances.

A sprite is 24 pixels by 21, 63 bytes, but most parts of a skater are smaller. The 477 shapes are stored with only the rows they use, three bytes to a row, and a copy routine fills the rest of the sprite with zeros as it builds it ($13FD). Packed that way, the shapes fill 16,428 bytes ($8284-$C2AF), where 477 full 64-byte sprite blocks would take 30,528.

The poses are stored facing one way. When the skater is on the left half of the ramp, the copy routines mirror each shape as they build the sprite: a single-colour row through a table that reverses the eight bits of a byte, and a multicolour row through a table that reverses the four pairs, so that the colours stay with their pixels ($138D, $13C5, tables at $2E7B and $2F7B, built when the event starts by $1340). The x offsets of the five parts change sign at the same time ($0C6F). The game builds the next frame's five sprites in one buffer while the VIC shows the other, and swaps them each frame ($14DE).

Sprite 4 is the board, and its colour comes from the skater, not the colour set: the event copies a byte the event manager keeps for each skater into both sets ($09EE). Sprite 7 is the board's shape again, in black, kept at the height of the coping while the skater is in the air ($0C29): it reads as the board's shadow. Sprites 5 and 6 are the black marks of the ruler at the top right, stretched to double height and copied into place when the event starts ($0A01). The yellow pole behind them is drawn into the bitmap, ten cells high, by $1AFA.

The shop and the town

Two bitmaps in two VIC banks

Rodney's shop and the town square are both multicolour bitmaps, kept in memory side by side. The shop's program switches between them by changing which 16 KB bank the VIC reads.

Drawn in the page from the shop's memory. The shop: the bitmap at $A000, the screen matrix at $8000, and the colour RAM image at $9C00. The town: the bitmap at $4000, the screen matrix at $6000, and the colour RAM image at $E000. Both on black. The pointer, the words in Rodney's balloon, and the town skater are sprites and are not part of the pictures.

The shop picture sits in VIC bank 2, with its bitmap at $A000 and its screen matrix at $8000 ($12AB). The town sits in bank 1, with its bitmap at $4000 and its screen matrix at $6000 ($12EC). In both, the screen byte gives two of each cell's colours and colour RAM the third. The town's pictures arrive packed in file $04 and the shop's bitmap in file $09, both with the same run-length code as the half-pipe ($0FEA). The shop's screen matrix, file $0A, is loaded as it is.

The program has one raster interrupt with two bands, at lines $FA and $2F ($209A). A mode byte at $26A4 says which screen is up, and the two bands set the VIC from it ($11EA, $12EC). The sign-in, records, and board-colour screens are not bitmaps: they are text, built in a buffer at $7000 from templates, in a font at $7800.

In the shop, the pointer is a sprite holding the words SKATE, OR, and DIE! in a tiny font, copied from $2641 to $8400. The balloon is part of the bitmap, and Rodney's words are drawn into six more sprites placed over it ($0EA9). In the town, the skater seen from above is five sprites, 0 to 4 ($13C0). The legs and board turn with the heading, and the body is picked a few steps ahead of them, so it leads into a turn.

The Downhill Race

A course of characters, scrolled a line at a time

The Downhill Race is drawn in multicolour text mode. The course is 255 rows of characters, of which the screen shows 22, and the course scrolls up a pixel line at a time as the skater rolls down it.

The 22 course rows of the screen, drawn in the page from the race's memory: characters from the map at $A600, colours from the low four bits of the map at $7E00, shapes from the character set at $4800, background grey and multicolours $0F and $0B ($1FC4). The window moves a pixel line each frame, a pace chosen for the page; in the game the course moves with the skater's speed. The three status rows above the course, the skater, and the overlays are left out.

The screen is at $5000 in VIC bank 1. Its top three rows are the status line, in a single-colour font at $4000; the 22 rows below are the course, in the course's own 256 characters at $4800. A cell whose colour has bit 3 set is multicolour, and the others are single-colour.

A raster interrupt in four bands splits the screen ($1F47). Band 0, in the bottom border at line $F6, sets up the status rows on blue ($1F84). Band 1, at line $45, sets the scroll and turns on multicolour and extended colour together ($1FF2). The VIC cannot show that mix, so it draws black, which hides the lines where the scroll changes. Band 2, at line $4D, waits a time that depends on the fine scroll and then sets the course's colours and character set ($1FC4). Band 3, at line $5A, redraws the course when needed ($2019).

The scroll position is a count of pixel lines. Divided by eight it gives the course row at the top of the screen, and the rest sets the VIC's fine scroll ($22E3). When the row changes, redraw_course copies 22 rows of characters to the screen and 22 rows of colours to colour RAM ($20A5). There is one screen, so the copy is done in one go, two rows at a time, by an unrolled loop. The flags, the skull, and the things in the lake move by rewriting their characters in the character set, so every copy of them on the course changes at once ($115C).

The riding skater is five sprites, each picked by the direction faced, one of 16, from banks at $393F: arms, head and torso, legs, board, and a wedge of shadow. The head and torso are multicolour. Falls and the other scripted actions are drawn from shapes kept under the I/O area and copied into four sprites at $7400 ($27D9). Eleven more sprites show parts of the course: the ends of the pipe, the flags, the hurdle bars, the arch, and the building.

The Downhill Jam

The same course drawing, with two screens

The Downhill Jam draws its course the way the race does, from a character map and a colour map, but it keeps two screens and draws the next one while the VIC shows the other.

The 22 course rows of the screen, drawn in the page from the Jam's memory: characters from the map at $8200, colours from the low four bits of the map at $5A00, shapes from the character set at $4800, background $0B and multicolours $0C and $00 ($23A9). Every eight frames the animated characters swap, as $0BA6 swaps them. The window moves a pixel line each frame, a pace chosen for the page.

The two screens are at $4400 and $5000 in VIC bank 1, and a byte at $3D0C says which is shown. The raster bands are those of the race, at lines $F6, $45, $4D, and $64 ($3563), with the same black lines from the impossible mode in band 1 ($233D). The playfield colours differ: dark grey behind, mid grey and black as the shared colours ($23A9).

The camera's row is its height divided by eight, and the rest is the fine scroll ($2891). While the fine scroll is below 4, copy_map_chars fills the hidden screen with the rows below ($25FF). When the camera reaches a new row, copy_map_colours writes the colours into colour RAM and flips the screens ($2742). Colour RAM has only one copy, so the colours change at the same moment as the screen.

Every eight frames, characters $40-$42 swap with $80-$82 and $60-$62 with $A0-$A2 ($0BA6). The police car's lights flash in colour RAM instead, at $DA9D and $DAED, while the camera is at the bottom ($29AC).

Each skater is three sprites: body, legs, and board. Player 0 takes sprites 5 to 7 and player 1 sprites 2 to 4, swapped each frame so the skater further down is drawn in front ($1004). The shapes are trimmed multicolour sprites under the I/O area, copied each frame into blocks at $5400 ($135C). Sprites 0 and 1 are shadows ($2BE9). Other sprites are the clothesline's shadow, a solid block that hides a skater on the secret path, the sparkle over a smashed can or bottle, and the tumbling bins.

The Pool Joust

A bitmap pool, and a second picture under the chips

The Pool Joust goes back to the half-pipe's way of drawing: a multicolour bitmap with a hires status row on top, and the skaters as sprites.

Drawn in the page from the joust's memory. The pool: the bitmap at $4000, the screen matrix at $6400, and the colours at $6000. The choice: the bitmap at $C000, the screen matrix at $E400, and the colours at $E000. Both on black. The pool's top row is drawn here in multicolour, as the bitmap holds it; the game shows that row hires, where its bytes read as black, until the names and scores are written into it.

Both pictures arrive packed with the half-pipe's run-length code ($2169). Each unpacks to 10,001 bytes at $8000: the bitmap, the colour-RAM nibbles, the screen matrix, and one spare byte. The pool is then copied to bank 1, with its bitmap at $4000 and its screen matrix at $6400. The choice picture is copied under the I/O chips, to $C000, with the chips switched out while the copy runs ($08CB). The processor cannot read RAM there while the chips are in, but the VIC always sees RAM, so the picture is shown from bank 3 ($0AD9). The colours go into colour RAM each time a picture is shown ($0AA0).

Two raster bands split the pool screen. Band 0, at line $FB, turns multicolour off so row 0 is drawn hires ($0BB6). Band 1, at line $38, waits for line $3A and turns it back on ($0D8B). The names, PRESS YOUR BUTTON, VS, and the scores are drawn by copying font glyphs into the bitmap cells of that row ($1427).

Each pose of a skater is four sprites: the pole, two for the body, and the board ($0D10). The pole, body, and board shapes come from 198 trimmed shapes, copied into buffers at $6C00 as stored for a skater facing right, or mirrored for one facing left ($15C9). The boards are 22 ready-made sprites, pre-mirrored, and are not copied. All sprites are multicolour, with light red and black as the shared colours ($0975). The pole's colour cycles through yellow, orange, and white while it swings, and the skater nearer the front gets the lower sprite numbers so it is drawn over the other ($0BDA).
