Commodore 64 · 1986 · Micro Power

Doctor Who and the Mines of Terror

The Sixth Doctor explores an alien mine with Splinx, a robot cat whose instructions you write yourself. Behind the adventure was a BBC Micro game that needed an extra ROM chip, followed by a Commodore version whose reviewers disagreed about almost everything except the music.

01 · The adventure

Into the mine

The Master, the Doctor’s renegade Time Lord adversary, has stolen plans for a machine that could interfere with the history of the universe. To power his Time Instant Replay Unit, he has taken over a heatonite mine on the second moon of Rijar. The Doctor’s mission is to recover the plans and stop production.813

The TARDIS deposits him in a cavern beside Splinx. From there, the mine scrolls in every direction through ladders, ledges, and machinery. The Doctor carries tools in four pockets, choosing what to take and what to leave for another journey. A spanner opens clamped ladders; a pick axe breaks through rock. Controllers patrol the passages, while a creature called the madrag guards her eggs.

Escape is the game’s only compulsory goal. The Time Lords judge what the Doctor achieves before leaving: recovering the plans and crystal, stopping the machine, and bringing Splinx home all improve his reward. Getting out alive can still mean returning from an unfinished mission.

02 · Splinx

Programming a companion

Splinx can follow the Doctor, wait where placed, or execute a program of up to ten commands. You place markers around the mine, then tell the cat which to visit, where to pick something up, and when to return. A battery limits how long Splinx can work before recharging.

The creatures’ behaviour gives those instructions a purpose. When the madrag finds an egg missing, she follows it and carries it back to her nest. Sending Splinx to take an egg draws her away, giving the Doctor time to reach the passage she guards.

Splinx also offers a way past the Master. He pursues the crystal when the Doctor carries it, even in a pocket, but leaves it alone when the cat carries it. The companion’s usefulness depends on understanding who reacts to what. The Gameplay page explains Splinx’s commands, and the Solution shows how to use them.911

03 · The release

From Castle Quest to Doctor Who

Gary Partis wrote the original BBC Micro game. In a 2007 interview with Stuart Goodwin, Partis recalled that Micro Power approached him shortly after his eighteenth birthday. The publisher wanted a Doctor Who game resembling Castle Quest, Tony Sothcott’s scrolling platform adventure for the BBC Micro. Partis worked on it during sixth form and his first months at Newcastle Polytechnic.4

The adventure exceeded the BBC Micro’s available memory. Repeated loading from cassette would have been impractical, Partis explained, so the 1985 release included a ROM chip that the player fitted inside the computer.43

The Commodore 64 and Amstrad CPC editions followed in 1986. On the C64, the title screen credits programming to Sothcott himself, and game design to Micro Power and Sothcott. Partis’s recollections describe the BBC original; the Commodore credits identify the people responsible for this edition.310

The project also put pressure on its publisher. Partis recalled that it caused Micro Power financial problems, partly because of the advertising. His account supports a more cautious conclusion than the later claim that the game’s development costs finished the company.47

A Spectrum edition was announced but never released. The C64 edition acquired a mistaken reputation for being unreleased too: preservation researcher Frank Gasking believed a Commodore Force feature that had included it among unreleased games, until he found an original copy.6

The source register records Partis’s interview, the release history, and the original disk’s credits.

04 · Reception

A large box and divided reviewers

The Commodore release cost £11.95 on cassette and £14.95 on disk. Its packaging included a map and a coloured code card, as well as extensive documentation. The card belonged to the adventure: a lock in the mine gives clues that the player uses to find a sequence of shapes on it.112

For Ken McMahon in Commodore User, the contents of the box raised expectations the game failed to meet. He found the adventure disappointing, struggled to make Splinx obey, and objected to the long pause after the Doctor lost a life.12

Zzap!64 reached a different judgment, awarding 86%. Its reviewers valued the depth of play more than the presentation of the mine itself. The reviewer initialled GL found it “enthralling and enjoyable to play” once the player became involved.14

Computer and Video Games praised the scale while finding the adventure ordinary. In Your Commodore, reviewer J.G. found the challenges “difficult but logical” and singled out the Doctor’s casual walking animation. Those judgments describe the same tension: a large place to explore, with puzzles whose appeal depended on whether their demands drew the player in.1513

The music attracted much less disagreement. Zzap!64 complained about its repetitive accompaniment and asked where the familiar television theme had gone. J.G. recommended playing with the volume turned off, despite recommending the game overall. The Music page plays the five in-game tunes.1413

05 · Inside the game

What the mine keeps hidden

Some puzzles become especially clear when their rules are visible. The heatonite machine starts with lettered circuits spelling START. Replacing two circuits makes the first four slots spell STOP, and the machinery comes to a halt. The map and solution show where those replacement letters lie.

Other rules are less helpful to the player. Bringing Splinx to the exit is insufficient for the rescue bonus: the Doctor must pick the cat up and carry it out. The game also keeps an unseen time bonus, but reduces it only once every 21.8 minutes.

The interactive mine map locates the objects and follows routes through the adventure. The Graphics page animates the Doctor and the TARDIS, while Discoveries explains the slow timer and the save that ends the session.
