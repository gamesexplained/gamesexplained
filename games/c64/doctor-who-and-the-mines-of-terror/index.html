Commodore 64 · 1986 · Micro Power

Doctor Who and the Mines of Terror

An arcade adventure in which the Sixth Doctor and Splinx, a robot cat the player programs, take on the Master in a mine on the second moon of Rijar.

01 · The adventure

The Doctor, a robot cat, and a mine full of heatonite

The Master has stolen the Doctor's plans for a TIRU, a Time Instant Replay Unit that would let its owner interfere with the history of the universe.8 A TIRU runs on heatonite, so the Master has taken over a heatonite mine on the second moon of the planet Rijar.1 The Time Lords send the Doctor to recover the plans, stop the mine, and get away.3

He will not have the mine to himself. Rijans live there, along with madrags and the Master's guard robots.28 The robots look like Daleks without being Daleks, because the Daleks' creator, Terry Nation, part-owned the rights to them.3 The game calls them controllers, and touching one ends with A SHOCK FROM A CONTROLLER (controller_touch, $CE08).

The Doctor explores one huge mine that scrolls in every direction, with four pockets for what he finds and five regenerations to spend. He does not go alone. Splinx, a robot cat, can be programmed to fetch what he cannot reach, and the aliens cannot detect it.1

02 · The release

A BBC Micro game too big for the BBC Micro

Gary Partis wrote the game for the BBC Micro. Micro Power asked him, shortly after he turned 18, to write a game like Tony Sothcott's Castle Quest, "a sideways scrolling platform game", but based on Doctor Who. He wrote it during sixth form and his first months at Newcastle Polytechnic.4 The BBC version came out in 1985 with a ROM chip to fit inside the computer, because the game was too big for the machine's memory.3 "The memory requirement was such that constant tape access (disks were not that common then) was impractical," Partis said.4

The Commodore 64 and Amstrad CPC versions followed in 1986.3 The Commodore version's title screen gives its programming to Tony Sothcott, the author of Castle Quest, and its design to Micro Power and Sothcott.10 The Commodore game cost £11.95 on cassette and £14.95 on disk.1 A ZX Spectrum version was announced and never released: in April 1986 Micro Power's Bob Simpson put the delay down to its programmer being pulled off to work on the other versions.6

Bob Simpson, a former accountant, had set up Micro Power in Leeds in the early 1980s. Its other Commodore 64 games included Bumble Bee, Cybertron Mission, Felix in the Factory, Frenzy, Ghouls, Jet Power Jack, Killer Gorilla, Stock Car, and Swoop.5 Micro Power promoted Mines of Terror heavily, and in 2013 The Register wrote that "the huge cost of its development did for Micro Power".7 Partis was more careful: "Certainly, it caused a financial problem with Micro Power; partially due to the advertising."4

Frank Gasking, who records unreleased games, long thought the Commodore version had never come out, because Commodore Force magazine had listed it by mistake in a feature on unreleased games. He later found an original copy.6

03 · Reception

86 % from Zzap!64, music and all

Zzap!64 reviewed the Commodore version in its thirteenth issue, dated May 1986, and gave it 86 % with presentation marked highest and sound lowest.31 Its reviewers liked the depth of play far more than the graphics and sound, and found it "very similar to Castle Quest". Once a player gets into it, "it proves enthralling and enjoyable to play", and it is "one of the best arcade adventures to appear on the 64". The music was the complaint: "The big niggle is the horrible music which burbles away throughout the game," one wrote, and another called it "the ubiquitous tune that migrainly rattles away while you play".1 The Music tab plays it.
