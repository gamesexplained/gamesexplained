1991 · Rainbow Arts · Commodore 64

Turrican II: The Final Fight

The suit’s weapons share damage machinery, while each world replaces the enemies and rules above it.

01 · game

Bren McGuire fights his way back

Bren McGuire survives the attack on the starship Avalon 1. Wearing a Turrican fighting suit, he crosses five worlds to confront the Machine. Most levels let him explore on foot; the third world sends him flying through three scrolling stages.

The suit carries upgradeable guns, a surrounding flash, and limited special weapons. Enemies take energy, while the timer and life counter limit each attempt. Eleven level files lead to the final fight and ending.

02 · music

Music: the introduction has its own player

The introduction keeps playing while the next files load. Its music uses a different driver from the title, flying stages, and ending. The choices below include every stored song selector. Seven introduction headers contain empty orders. The second world-3-3 selector loops a pattern with instrument zero. Those eight choices are labelled silent.

The page runs the game’s own music players on the kit’s 6502 processor and sends their writes to the SID sound-chip model. Tests compare every register write for every choice with the original driver calls. The 6581 filter uses one measured chip’s response; the switch lets it be heard without that filter. Some low notes are hard to hear on small speakers.

03 · effects

Sound: the weapons have separate effect patterns

Ordinary gameplay has a three-voice effects interpreter. The ending has a second copy with seven nonzero patterns. These buttons identify the stored pattern numbers; a number can serve several events.

The gameplay player starts at $6290. Pattern zero is silent; patterns 28–31 share one source. The ending player starts at $8891. The players initialize volume before each button starts.

04 · weapons

The flash borrows the projectile damage table

Holding fire charges the surrounding flash. At full charge, twenty records follow selected directions around the suit. The effect borrows eighteen damage entries and bitmap space, then restores both when it finishes. That lets the ordinary enemy hit tests recognize the flash through the same damage lookup as projectiles.

Shared sources: $3931, $3A37, and the weapon levels at $07EE through $07F0. Flying stages replace parts of this weapon code. Their pieces appear on the Graphics tab.

05 · terrain

A map byte chooses a four-by-four block

The scrolling world is built from reusable squares. Each square contains sixteen character numbers. The map stores the square’s index with its two four-bit halves exchanged.

Some maps also hold boss tables or executable helpers between terrain rows. The Levels tab draws all eleven rectangles and marks those allocations separately. The Source tab names their individual purposes.

Definitions begin at $B000. The shared drawing routines select a definition by shifting the stored byte into its address. A single map block covers 32 by 32 pixels.

The controller paths begin at $1063, $2DBE, $2F95, and $42B1. The pause branch reads bit two of the keyboard input at $0BAC; its resume branch reads joystick fire. The feature ledger distinguishes traced paths from live input checks.

07 · scores

Fifty diamonds buy a continue

The score uses packed decimal digits, so its bytes behave like the digits on the panel. Separate requests let an enemy ask for points without drawing the score immediately. Diamonds follow a similar request path: the panel consumer adds them, and reaching fifty supplies another continue.

After the last fight, the ending converts remaining resources into points. The conversion rates are shown below.

The shared decimal consumer starts at $0EE0. The ending conversion begins at $7CA1. The title writes ten high-score entries into a raw disk sector.

08 · bosses

Large bosses reuse the same hardware sprites

The Commodore 64 has eight hardware sprites. A large boss needs more pieces than that, so the game changes the sprites’ shapes and positions as the display moves down the screen. The first boss draws three columns across five bands. Later bosses use different column counts and body layouts.

Boss damage is tested separately from that display. A hit can depend on its row, side, pose, and current phase. One final-boss scan misses a comparison between adjacent cells; the Discoveries tab shows the instruction trace.

Level 1-1’s body and hit paths are in its listing. Every part has its own Source page because the same memory addresses hold different code after a load.
