1989 · Alternative Software · Commodore 64

Count Duckula

Forty-eight rooms that fit together into one pyramid, and a clock that loses a minute for every frame a mummy touches the Count.

01 · Count Duckula

A saxophone before dawn

Count Duckula has brought his castle to Egypt to find a magic saxophone in the pyramid of the Pharaoh Upanatem. At dawn the castle flies back to Transylvania, with him or without him. The clock in the panel counts down the hours and minutes he has left, from 9:59 to 0:00.

He walks, jumps and climbs stairs, opens doors with keys, and carries up to three objects. Mummies, falling rocks, bats and the Crow Brothers wander the corridors. None of them can kill him. They take his time instead.

02 · Count Duckula

Music: the television theme

The game’s own music player, playing its only tune on the C64’s SID chip.

The theme never stops. The same interrupt that moves Duckula calls the music player once a frame, from the title screen to the last screen, and the game has no sound effects to interrupt it.

The player keeps a list of patterns for each of its three voices, and each pattern is a list of notes, each with an instrument that shapes its attack, waveform and pitch. The player can start any of seven tunes, but this game uses only the first: the other six entries in its table point into the first tune’s own lists and into the room maps.

03 · Count Duckula

Forty-eight rooms make one pyramid

Every room, drawn from the game’s room maps in its place in the grid. Choose a room to open it on the Maps tab.

Each room fills one screen, but the rooms fit together into one building. The game keeps a grid thirteen squares wide and six high, and 48 of the squares hold a room: three on the top floor, then five, seven, nine, eleven and thirteen. The empty squares are outside the pyramid. Walking off the side of a room moves Duckula one square across, and leaving by the top or bottom moves him a whole row.

The castle stands in the bottom left square, where the game begins. Each room is twenty tiles across and ten down, and each tile is a 16-by-16-pixel picture, so the stairs and corridors line up from one room to the next.

04 · Count Duckula

Every frame a mummy touches Duckula costs a minute

The second room of the game, its mummy and Duckula, with the game’s own clock panel, at the game’s speed of 50 frames a second.

The clock loses a minute every seventy frames, a little under a second and a half, so the night lasts about fourteen minutes of play.

The manual says a mummy, a rock or a crow freezes Duckula and costs him an hour. The game does neither. Each frame that an enemy overlaps him takes another minute off the clock, and he turns red for fourteen frames, but he keeps walking. A mummy that walks through him while he stands still costs nine minutes. Walking into it costs five, because the two pass each other twice as fast. Bats, which the manual says take points, take minutes like the rest.

Two things give time back: the ankh, and a visit from Igor. Neither works the way the manual says, and the Discoveries tab shows both.

The objects drawn from their sprites, in the colours and bobbing motion the game gives them. Room numbers are squares of the grid, counted from the top left; the Maps tab shows where each one lies.

Duckula carries three objects and uses them with the keys 1, 2 and 3. Picking up a fourth drops the oldest at his feet, and he can pick nothing up for three seconds afterwards, so he can walk away from it.

06 · Count Duckula

The winning screen spells IGOR as NDOR

The screen the game draws when Duckula wins.

Using the large key at the keyhole at the top of room 6 ends the game at once, and the theme plays on under this screen.

Its second line is 34 letters long. The loop that draws it works out where each letter goes in a single byte, at eight bytes of picture a letter, and a byte only counts to 255. Past the 32nd letter it starts again from nought, so the N and D of AND are drawn over the I and G of IGOR.
