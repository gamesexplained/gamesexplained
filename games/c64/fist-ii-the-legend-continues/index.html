Commodore 64 · 1986 · Beam Software

Fist II: The Legend Continues

Eight scrolls rewrite what the hero can do: one narrows a crevice so he can somersault across, another makes the drop below the volcano survivable. Beam Software turned the Exploding Fist duels into a quest.

01 · The adventure

Beyond the dojo

A young disciple sets out to free a land ruled by the Warlord. The masters of the Exploding Fist have hidden their knowledge on eight scrolls, each belonging to a meditation chamber somewhere in the realm. Finding a scroll is only half the task. You must carry it to its chamber before its power becomes yours. The manual supplies the story and hints at what each scroll teaches.

The world opens as you learn. Stone heaps that once blocked a path can be smashed; trees can be kicked down. Other scrolls bring light to dark rooms or protection against poisonous chambers. Exploring means remembering places you could not pass and finding your way back with the knowledge they require.

The Warlord’s men interrupt that exploration with one-to-one fights. Joystick directions produce punches, sweeps, and kicks, with the fire button changing the move. Energy carries over between encounters and slowly recovers. Meditation refills the bar and raises its capacity with every opponent beaten since the game began. The manual says “since your last meditation”, but the code never resets the count (details).

02 · The makers

Making a different sequel

Melbourne studio Beam Software had already found success with Gregg Barnett’s The Way of the Exploding Fist, twelve weeks at the top of the UK chart.11 For its sequel, Barnett later recalled, “it would have been easy to just reskin the original game”. His recollection, quoted by Super Chart Island, places that familiar option beside the much larger adventure Beam made.

Programmer Nigel Spencer described the result as the original fighting engine combined with a platform game. His C64.com interview also recalls the screen editor and the work of fitting the levels into memory. The landscape needed tools for arranging it as well as artists to draw it.

The British cassette manual credits Barnett with design and programming, Greg Holland with backgrounds and scenery, and Russel Comte with character animation and sprites. It credits Nigel Spence, Trevor Nuridin, and Bruce Bayley with “graphic arrangements”. The tools receive their own recognition: David Johnston’s Character Editor and Nigel Spencer’s Map Editor. The manual prints Spence in the arrangements credit and Spencer beside the editor.1

Neil Brennan composed the music, while Bayley supplied the sound effects. Brennan’s account of working at Beam describes cofounder Fred Milgrom discussing characters, artwork, and the musical feeling he wanted before Brennan developed themes on a four-track recorder. That interview describes his broader working method rather than Fist II specifically. In Fist II, three tunes accompany different parts of the world; all three can be heard here.

03 · The reception

A divided reception

Melbourne House published the British release as Fist II: The Legend Continues. Mindscape’s American edition shortened the title to Fist: The Legend Continues.7 The Mindscape disk is the edition examined on these pages.

Fist II topped the combined UK formats chart for one week in October 1986. Contemporary C64 reviewers disagreed over the sequel. Commodore User’s Ken McMahon expected “the definitive Kung Fu classic”, while Your Commodore welcomed the decision to build a quest around the combat. Zzap!64’s Julian Rignall thought the game unfinished and overpriced; fellow reviewer Gary Penn called it “basically a big disappointment”. Super Chart Island collects those reviews and the chart record.

A separate Training program retains organised bouts, including two-player competition.9 This site follows the Adventure.

04 · Inside the game

What the scrolls change

The scrolls’ cryptic promises become more concrete when matched to the game’s rules. One supposedly grants calm and poise. Its practical effect is to narrow a crevice enough for the hero to somersault across. Another offers protection from danger: without that knowledge, dropping through the floor below the volcano empties the hero’s energy. With it, the drop becomes the entrance.↗

Those rules explain why a familiar-looking place can become passable later. They also give the map a purpose beyond showing where rooms connect.
