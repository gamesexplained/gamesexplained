Commodore 64 · 1986 · Beam Software

Fist II: The Legend Continues

One tune for each part of the world, played by the game’s own driver, where a slip means only the first voice restarts its vibrato. The sound effects are samples, clicked out through the volume register.

Music

The tunes are by Neil Brennan.1

Three tunes, one for each part of the world

The game plays one tune at a time, chosen by where the hero stands ($1247), and loops it for as long as he stays. Press a tune to hear it.

The game's music driver ($F400) ported to JavaScript and played through the site's model of the SID chip. The port was checked against the driver itself, run in a 6502 simulator on the game's bytes: all 25 sound registers after each of 30,000 frames, ten minutes of each tune, match. Tune 1 sweeps the filter, so the player starts with the filter of a 6581, the C64's first sound chip; its cutoff is one chip's, and real 6581s differ widely. The filter sweep follows voice 3's envelope, which the model reads once a frame where the chip would be read part-way through it.

Brennan has described how he worked at Beam: Fred Milgrom, one of the studio's two founders,6 talked him through a game's characters and artwork and "the kind of musical 'feel' that he was after", and Brennan would "head off to my little 4-track to put down as many themes as I could muster". Milgrom's "view of games was very cinematic", he says: "He would make me watch Fellini films, or study Chinese music".14

The driver

Sequences, patterns and 21-byte instruments

The driver ($F400) runs once a frame from the status-band interrupt. Each voice reads a sequence ($F5BD): play pattern n, repeat a block, or jump back to loop. A pattern is a list of notes, each a note byte ($F68E) (the note in the low four bits, the octave in the next three) and a length byte ($F6C0) that picks one of sixteen lengths ($F4D5). The notes come from a table of one octave ($F4C8), doubled for each octave up. A byte of $80 or more in a pattern changes the instrument ($F683).

There are 17 instruments of 21 bytes ($FE43). An instrument sets the waveform and envelope, and can sweep the pulse width from one of three ramps, glide from note to note, add a vibrato or a trill, and route the voice through the filter with a cutoff that sweeps. Only voice 1 restarts its vibrato with each new note: the instruction that asks for the restart ($F6F0) is not indexed by voice, so voices 2 and 3 keep theirs running.

Sound effects

Samples through the volume register

The sound effects are samples, made by Bruce Bayley, the manual says.1 A timer interrupt ($3D8B) switches the SID's volume between two levels, and each byte of a sample sets how long until the next switch, through a table of 256 timer periods ($0200). Each switch of the volume makes a click, and the clicks at the right spacing make the sound.

Each sample has a rate and three counts in a table of four bytes a sample ($BEC6), and its start and end in a second table ($FFCB). The rate sets how fast the interrupt’s timer counts. The counts shape the loudness, once a frame ($3E1A): it rises from 1 to 15, holds at 15 while the sample repeats, and falls back to 1 ($3F00). The first and last counts set how slowly it rises and falls, and a count of 0 skips that stage; the middle one sets how many more times the sample plays at full loudness.

The sample player ($3D8B, $3DB8, $3E1A) is ported to JavaScript, and the port was checked against the game’s own routines run in a 6502 simulator on the game’s bytes: every write to the volume register, and the frame and cycle it falls on, match for all ten. The timers are modelled from what the routines write to them, not emulated cycle by cycle. Samples 1 and 5 use the same bytes, as do 2, 6, and 9, and 4 and 8; each plays them at its own rate and loudness. Sample 10’s start and end lie past the last of the other nine, where the loaded bytes run into $FF and $00 ($FFEF), so it plays $C840-$C9CF, which holds the high bytes of the timer table and what follows. Whether that is meant is not known.
