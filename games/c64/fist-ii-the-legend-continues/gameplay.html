Commodore 64 · 1986 · Beam Software

Fist II: The Legend Continues

How the game is played: the quest, the joystick, the energy bar, the eight scrolls and the enemies who come back, from the manual and from the code.

Words underlined with dots open the code they describe on the Source code tab.

The quest

Find eight scrolls, then the Warlord

You play a young disciple, descended from a Grand Master of the Exploding Fist, sent by the village elders "to find and defeat the evil Warlord" in his volcano fortress. That is the manual's story; the game makes it a walk across 123 joined screens with a fight wherever someone is waiting.

The masters wrote their secrets on scrolls and scattered them. Each scroll belongs to a temple, where the hero meditates on the trigram printed on the altar mat; with the matching scroll he learns what it teaches, and that opens a way somewhere else. The volcano lies past several of those obstacles, and its last room starts the ending. The Maps and solution tab has the whole world and a route through it.

The manual quoted here is the C64 documentation as Lemon64 keeps it, read on 1 October 2026.

Controls

Eight directions, with and without fire

The joystick in port 2 does everything. Each direction means one thing with the fire button up, and another with it pressed. The diagrams are the manual's, drawn for a fighter facing right: "forward" is the way he faces.

To turn round, start a roundhouse and let go of the button. Blocking is automatic: step back just as the opponent is about to land a blow, and the hero blocks high or low as the blow needs, so holding a block does not work. The kneeling punch, "the true Exploding Fist punch", is a crouch followed by a push forward. RESTORE pauses the game and F5 ends it.

In the code, a table turns the joystick position and the fire button into a move, and a second says which moves may follow which. The forward somersault is how the hero clears a hole or a drop: it carries him about ten columns.

Chi

One energy bar for the whole journey

The manual calls energy Chi. The hero's bar is the scroll at the bottom left, the opponent's at the bottom right, and the hero's carries over from fight to fight.

WhatHow it works
Starting energy47, which is also the bar's limit to begin with
Getting it backone point every 128 frames, about 2.5 seconds, up to the limit
Meditatingfills the bar and sets its limit to 47 + 4 for every two opponents beaten in the whole game, at most 127
Livesone to start with, one more for each scroll delivered, one lost at each defeat; the hero starts again at the last temple he meditated in
Hazardscost 2 or 10 energy; a blow at the right moment knocks one away

The manual says the limit grows "depending on the number of opponents you have defeated since your last meditation". The code counts every defeat since the game began instead: the counter is cleared only when a game starts, and meditating reads it without clearing it. So the limit reaches 127 after twenty defeats and stays there, wherever the hero meditates. The counter itself stops at 31.

Scrolls

Eight scrolls, eight trigrams

Each scroll carries a trigram, three lines whole or broken, and the manual gives each a hint "as to how and when their knowledge can be used". The status band shows the trigram of every scroll found. Matching the game's icons to the manual's drawings puts a name to each scroll, and the code says what each one actually does.

The icons are drawn from the game's status-band font, two characters per scroll from two small tables, top line first. Eight bytes record the scrolls: 1 when a scroll is found, $80 once it is delivered. The manual lists the trigrams in a different order (Ch'ien, Sun, Chen, Kan, Ken, K'un, Li, Tui); the names here follow the lines, not the list. Where each scroll lies and the way to its temple are on the Maps and solution tab.

Enemies

The Warlord's men, and hordes of them

The manual names peasant soldiers, warrior guards, ninjas who throw stars, masked shoguns, assassins who "cannot be outrun", and mud warriors from the caves, with snakes, panthers and bats besides. Fighting is optional: "you need not fight them to continue your exploration ... but you will not gain beneficial experience".

Opponents fight with attack combinations, short scripts of moves chosen from a jump table and a random number. How a blow is judged to land is below.

Blows

How a blow is judged to land

Whether a blow lands is worked out from the move and the frame of its animation, not from the pictures touching.

For each move and each frame, a table gives either $80, no strike in this frame, or a value the game compares with the distance between the two fighters. There are two tables, one for fighters facing each other and one for both facing the same way, so a kick behind you is judged by its own numbers. The hardware's sprite collision register is read as well. A blow that lands scores 100 points times the move's value.

A move is a script of four-byte steps: how long to hold, which pose, and how far to move across and up, read one step at a time. There are 44 scripts. The opponent's combinations are picked with a random number from an 8-bit shift register.

Late game

After thirty defeats, the dead come back

Every place where an opponent waits is a record in a table of 54, and beating him marks the record done. A done record stays quiet until the hero has beaten 30 opponents. From then on, each time the game checks a done opponent's place as the hero reaches it, it rolls a random number and brings him back one time in four.

A player on Lemon64 described the same thing from play: enemies "start respawning later in the game", tied to "a kill count threshold" rather than to the scrolls; an opponent scrolled off the screen before he is beaten is still there on the way back; and in the late game a returning opponent "may re-appear, or he may not". The code agrees on all three. The same player's claim that the fifth trigram has "no measurable effect" fits too, as far as damage goes: scroll 5's only reader is the horde check above, so it never touches a blow's strength. It shortens hordes instead.

The player tested the cassette version; this site's reading is of the Mindscape disk. The rules match what the player saw, but the two releases have not been compared byte for byte.

Tournament

A second game on the disk, for practice

The manual describes a Tournament mode for one or two players, against "a series of computer opponents or a friend", with keyboard controls for both fighters (F7 switches between keyboard and joystick). The Mindscape disk offers it as Training, beside the Adventure. It is a separate program, and this site covers only the Adventure.
