A boy walks through five haunted screens to find his girlfriend, then leads her back through the same five, left exactly as he left them.

01 · The ten screens

Five screens out, the same five back

Each screen is built from one level record: a play area, a character set, a colour map, five enemies and ten crosses. The picture below is drawn by this page from those bytes, not taken from a screenshot. Pick a screen, and switch on the tiles the boy can stand on, the ledges that crumble and the pick-ups.

A record is $80 bytes, and the ten sit at $7000-$757F, listed by level_records at $7290. setup_screen at $5E19 reads one field after another: the play area (screen rows 2 to 24) at +0, the character set at +6, the colour map at +$0C, packed two cells to a byte, and the two shared multicolours at +$0E. It then places the crosses from the ten addresses at +$5E (place_crosses, $5FC0). The five outward screens keep their graphics at $8000-$B1FF, one $A00 block each.

02 · The way back

Secret: the way back is the screen you left

The five return screens have no play areas of their own. As the boy leaves each screen on the way out, the game copies it away, and on the way back it reads that copy. A mushroom he ate, a cross he took, a ledge that crumbled under him: all still gone.

The copy is made by next_screen ($7680): screen rows 1 to 24 go to a store chosen by screen_store at $7FE8. The forest goes to $7800. The other four have no room left in ordinary memory, so they go into the RAM under the KERNAL ROM, at $E000, $E400, $E800 and $EC00. A write there lands in the RAM even with the ROM switched in. Reading it back needs the ROM switched out, which is the only thing copy_under_kernal ($72A4) does: it sets the processor port $01 to $34, copies, and puts it back.

Coming back, each screen needs all ten crosses, five blue and five red. The outward records list only five, so the red ones were never on the screen going out; the way-back record lists all ten and place_crosses writes them into the stored copy. The page's screen builder above shows the way-back screens over the untouched outward screens, which is what a player sees who takes only the crosses.

Starting a game straight on a way-back screen shows what the store holds before anything has been saved there. Poking the start level at $C661 to 10 does that, and the house comes up as garbage: whatever the loader left under the KERNAL.

03 · Music: the play tune, the card tune and the jingle

Three tunes from a twelve-command player

The music is played by the game's own driver, ported line by line and run through a model of the sound chip. Press a tune.

The port was checked against the game's driver running in a 6502 simulator on the game's memory: every sound-chip write of 9,000 interrupts of the play tune, and of the card tune and the jingle to their ends, is the same in the same order. What you hear is close to what the C64 plays, not the same. The model plays PAL timing. The game sets the sound chip's filter once and leaves it on for the whole tune (music_init_filter, $60A0, the only writes to $D415-$D418 in the game): cutoff 557 of 2047 ($D415 = $05, $D416 = $45), resonance 15 with only voice 1 routed through it, low-pass and band-pass both selected. The Filter switch plays the tune three ways, and changes it while it plays; mute voice 2 to hear voice 1, the bass, alone. Off leaves the filter out. 6581 is the chip every C64 had in 1984, on the cutoff curve the reSID emulator (version 0.16) measured from one such chip: 557 lands near 510 Hz, so voice 1 loses its upper harmonics (15 to 28 dB quieter above 2.4 kHz) and gains a resonant hump between 300 and 600 Hz. 8580, the chip of later C64s, puts the same setting near 3,600 Hz, and voice 1 barely changes. Real 6581s differ from one another, and at this setting one chip's cutoff can sit well above or below another's; they also distort, which this model does not (site/lib/sid.js).

The driver runs on the system interrupt, 60 times a second, and uses two voices. Each command is one byte, and the byte is the low half of the address of its handler in page $61: music_next_command ($6100) writes it into its own JMP at $610B and jumps. So the tune data is a list of jump targets.

The count command is how the music talks to the game. The card before each screen stays up until the card tune counts (card_wait, $7610), and GAME OVER stays up until the jingle does ($5DA9). The play tune never counts; it loops after about 69 seconds.

The tune's pitches are worked out for an American machine. Of its 478 frequency values, 464 land within two cents of a note at the NTSC clock. On a European PAL machine, which runs about 4 % slower, every note plays about a third of a semitone flat, and that is what this player plays.

04 · Two players, two colours

The boy takes blue crosses, the girl takes red

A cross is one character, tile $57. Whose it is depends only on its colour. Pick a character and a cross colour and see what cross_touch does.

touches a

cross_touch ($7F50) reads the colour-RAM cell under the cross and tests bit 2. Light blue is 14 (%1110) and light red is 10 (%1010), so the bit tells them apart. A match replaces the cross with a space and steps the MAGIC CROSSES counter (cross_count, $5B5E). Anything else is left alone. The crosses also flash: every 40th pass colour_cycle ($7F80) takes 8 from each cross's colour, which toggles the multicolour bit and back.

Going out only the boy plays, and each screen needs its five blue crosses. On the way back the girl comes too, and both colours are needed, ten in all. Fire, or the ? key, swaps who is under control, but switch_allowed ($7280) refuses before level byte 10, the first screen of the way back, exactly as the inlay's starred note says. The border shows who is playing: tick_border ($75B0) resets it every pass to 6, blue, for the boy and 10, light red, for the girl.

A swap does not move anyone. player_swap ($576B) exchanges sprites 0 and 1, positions and all, so whoever is being controlled is always sprite 0, and patches the frame numbers in three routines to the other character's shapes. The one left behind stands still and still loses energy when an enemy touches it (girl_touch, $CD9D).

Secret: three crosses cannot be taken. A search of every screen against try_move's own rules (work/reach.py) finds nine of the ten screens fully collectable, and three blue crosses that are not, all on the way back, all in the play area's bottom two rows: graveyard-back, ghetto-back, cinema-back. move_sprite's Y clamp ($C930, cmp #$E3) holds the boy's own position at row 22 of 24 even in free fall over open ground with nothing to land on, so his sprite can never be computed as standing in those rows, whatever the scenery says. See the Maps / levels tab to check each screen for yourself.

05 · Energy

One bar, no lives, and standing still is free

There is one energy bar and nothing else to lose. Walking wears it down, enemies and toadstools poison it, mushrooms fill it. Try them on the bar below, which uses the game's own step sizes.

Drawn with the game's own bar glyphs, $A1-$A9 of the play character set, in the colours of the forest's colour map: 33 cells of eight steps. When the bar is empty the game is over.

The inlay promises that walking, running and jumping each cost more than the last. There is no running in the code: one walking speed, and it gets slower. Once the bar's seventh cell is no longer full, bar_speed ($5961) patches the main loop to read the controls every 128 passes instead of every 32.

Lives are in the code but not in the game. new_life ($CAED) and lose_life ($CEDA) and a starting count at $45ED are there, reached only from other code that nothing calls. An empty bar goes straight to GAME OVER (out_of_energy, $5A70).

06 · The jump

Secret: no fall ever hurts

The jump is 24 one-pixel steps up, each a little slower than the last, and the same steps back down in reverse. Scrub through it. However far the boy falls afterwards, he lands unhurt.

Height against time from jump_speeds at $544A: the number of main-loop passes before each step. The boy's sprite is his jumping frame, pointer $E0, drawn from the game's sprite data at $3800.

Up on the stick, or SHIFT, starts a jump (start_jump, $5418) unless he is already falling. In the air the controls are switched off by writing an RTS over the first byte of read_controls ($C84D), so the direction he took off in is kept until he lands. There is no climbing: on all ten screens up and down are switched off as walking directions ($4500-$4503), so the only way up is to jump.

Falls are counted. When he lands, land ($53EF) compares the count with a limit at $457A, and the branch after the compare was replaced with NOPs. The limit is $FF anyway. Fall damage was written and then switched off twice over.

The ground has one more trick: tiles $4D-$53 are a ledge in seven stages. Every eighth step on one moves it a stage further, and the last stage becomes empty space (tile_touch_a, $5A9A). Stand on a crumbling ledge too long and it drops you.

07 · The enemies

Five enemies a screen, faster every LEVEL

Each screen has five enemy slots on sprites 2 to 6, and a sixth enemy on sprite 7 that is thrown in from one of them now and then. Their shapes come from the level record. Flip through each screen's set.

Four frames per slot, in the order the game steps through them, drawn from the sprite data the record's frame table names. Colours are those the game set on the screen ($D029-$D02D, read live on arrival); multicolour sprites use $D025 and $D026.

Each slot has a number of lives ($45E7) and a chance out of 128 of coming back after it leaves the screen ($45E2, respawn_timer at $2FB2). When every slot has used all its lives, level_up ($2C19) counts the LEVEL counter on and shows it over the play area, and speed_up ($5681) halves every slot's movement and animation delays. So the longer the boy lingers, the faster the enemies get.

A touch costs energy and nothing else. sprite_touch ($CE87) reads the hardware collision register $D01E; when sprite 0 and exactly one enemy are in it, two steps of poison go on the bar. The enemy stays. In one test run, standing still from the start of the forest, the bar held for 16 seconds and then lost 69 steps in a single visit, all of them from enemy touches.

Each slot moves along a path script: direction bytes, 0 up, 1 down, 2 left, 3 right, ended by $FF, at path_scripts ($4A00-$4A8F). path_step ($CC12) moves the enemy one pixel every few passes and reads the next byte of its script every 8th move; $FF loops back to the start. Nine scripts serve the ten screens' fifty slots: three walk down then back up, four hold one direction for ever, and three are long runs down with a single stray byte the move routine simply does nothing with, since it matches none of the four directions it checks for. Step through any slot's script on the Maps / levels tab.

08 · Controls

The keys go through the machine's own keyboard scan

The game reads the joystick itself, but leaves the keyboard to the Commodore's built-in routines and reads the key they found.

10 · Bugs

Bug: PRESS CTRL FOR MENU never appears

After GAME OVER the game calls a routine to put PRESS CTRL FOR MENU on the bottom row. It reads the text, reads the screen, reads the colours, and writes nothing.

The text at $574A, drawn here as it would have looked. show_press_ctrl at $5736 loops 19 times over LDA $574A,X, LDA $07CA,X, LDA $D805, LDA $DBCA,X: four loads, no store.

Two smaller slips. The jump sound should rise again as he falls: jump_pitch_fall ($5518) adds 5 to the frequency's low byte, and its carry test at $5524 branches to the very next instruction, so the high byte is taken down by one every step and the pitch keeps dropping. And on the title after a game, title_flicker ($7580) should flicker two colour registers, $D022 and $D023; its second store goes to $7523, a byte in the last level record, so only one flickers.
