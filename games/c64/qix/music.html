1989 · Taito America

Qix

The eleven tunes and twelve sound effects of the C64 Qix, played by the game's own two players running in this page.

01 · The music

Music: eleven tunes, one frame in six skipped

The game stores eleven three-voice tunes. The title plays the longest; the others are shuffled, and one plays at the end of each level. The Internet Archive's recording of this music credits Tim Follin.

The tunes played by the game's own music player: its bytes from $E000, run once a frame inside the page and heard through the site's model of the SID. The player reads three zero-page bytes before it writes them, so the first frame of a tune depends on what the game left there; the page starts from the title's values.

The player is a copy: the game loads it at $4000 and copies the 8,000 bytes to $E000 before the title and before each level's end tune ($884C). Play reuses $E000 as one of its three planes, so the music has to be put back each time.

The interrupt calls the player 60 times a second on an American NTSC machine and 50 times on a European PAL one. To keep the tempo the same, the start-up checks which machine it is on and patches two bytes of the player before the copy ($8030). On NTSC they make the player count six frames and skip one; on PAL the count never skips. Each voice reads a stream of notes and 21 commands (repeats, calls, jumps, transposition, waveform, filter, vibrato and sweeps), dispatched through a table per voice ($ED2B).

The program itself never names a composer. Play keeps its sound effects in a second, smaller player of its own (section 02), so the tune player is needed only between levels and on the title.

02 · The sound effects

Sound: twelve effects, and the Qix's hum tells you how it will move

Every noise in play comes from a sound-effect player at $B800, separate from the music. It stores twelve effects; the game plays eleven.

The game's own effect player, running in this page. Each button starts an effect as the game does, with the player's reset ($B800) and its request entry ($B806), then runs its frame routine ($B803) once a frame, as the play interrupt does, and plays what it writes through the site's model of the SID. The two hums and the Fuse repeat until the game starts another effect; stop them with the player's stop button.

Each effect and the code that asks for it, from every call to play_sfx ($8814) in the program. Lengths in frames, 60 a second on the American machine this copy runs on.

An effect is a list, for each voice it uses, of notes and commands (sfx_scripts, $BA9C). A note picks one of sixteen pitches and holds it for the voice's duration. Seven commands end the voice, set its waveform, its envelope, its duration or a pitch slide, jump back to repeat, or start another effect (sfx_read, $B951); the player applies the slide each frame (sfx_voice, $B8BF). The hums and the Fuse end in a jump back, so they repeat until the game asks for something else. An effect asked for during a frame waits in one of two slots until the next frame (sfx_request, $B885).

When the timer bar runs out, the game plays the alarm and locks it in for 255 frames, about four seconds (sfx_lock, $8813, set at $86A0). Until the lock runs down, the game drops any new effect except silence, the game's start, effect 2, the alarm and a death, so fills and the Qix's hum go unheard under the alarm.

The Qix's hum carries information. Each time the Qix draws a new turn time, its step picks effect 3 for a turn due within 4 steps and effect 4 for a longer one, and starts the hum only when the choice changes ($94DB). On level 1 every turn is due within 4 steps, so the hum never changes. From level 2, when the hum changes pitch, the Qix has started one of its long straight runs (How it works). A fill sounds slow or fast by how the line ended, not by how it was drawn: after_claim plays effect 8 only for a line that ended with fire released, and effect 7 for every line once a Spritz has been walled in.

Effect 2 is never asked for: Discoveries.
