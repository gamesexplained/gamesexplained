The two switches beside the screen change how the page draws the 3D view, the window in the middle of the castle's frame, and nothing else. The game runs underneath as it does without them: two copies of it, one with the view watching it, keep every byte of memory the same at every frame.

Native resolution

The game draws the view into a buffer of 120 by 120 pixels, each twice as wide as it is tall on the screen, and copies the buffer into the bitmap when the picture is done. Many of its colours are patterns: two colours in alternating pixels, which the screen blurs into a shade between them.

With Native resolution on, the page works each picture out again from what the game keeps for it: where the player stands and which way they look, and the records of the area's objects. It applies the game's own rules to them: the objects the game queued for the picture, each one's corners from its record, each face's colour from the side of it the player is on or from the way it winds, the faces that turn away left out by the game's own test on the corners as the game rounds them, and the order from farthest to nearest by the game's own bubble sort on the same bounds. Then it draws the same faces as sharp shapes at the screen's own resolution, each pattern as the shade the eye mixes from it, with the sky, the floor and the Wilderness's mountains where the game puts them, and the sight in the middle in the colours the game's XOR gives it.

The pictures are the 1,633 the game drew in 82,600 frames of play, from the loader's jump and from 199 moments in every area, walking, turning and looking up and down. The game works out each corner in whole numbers and drops the fraction toward the middle of the view. The page works it out exactly, which moves a corner by less than 2 pixels. The 60 faces that are further out are all cut by an edge of the view, where the game's cut and the page's put a corner up to 35 pixels apart; why is not known.

Smooth motion

The game shows a new picture only when one pass of its main loop has worked the scene out, sorted it, drawn it into the buffer and copied it to the screen. Over the walks above it drew 1,633 pictures in 82,600 frames, about one a second, and the screen shows each until the next is done.

With Smooth motion on, the page draws the view at the screen's own frame rate, 60, 120 or 144 times a second. The page runs the game a pass at a time, ahead of the frame on show, so the next picture is known before the C64 would show it. Each frame of the screen draws the view part of the way from the picture on show to the next: the player's place slid, the turn and the look turned the shorter way round, and an object that moved between them slid with them. The view gets to each picture at the frame the C64 first shows it: at the 1,253 pictures that came on show over the walks, it was on average 1.4 % of a step behind.

What the game changes at once stays sudden: another area, or a jump of more than 1,024 of the game's units. The viewport's opening and the menus are the game's own frames, and when the view comes to rest it is the game's own picture. Without Native resolution the page paints each view between two pictures in the C64's pixels with its own painter, which follows the game's: each pattern row by row, the sky and the floor, the mountains, the lightning and the sight. At the game's own pictures it matches the game's drawing in 99.16 % of the view's pixels; the rest are at the edges of faces, a pixel either way.
