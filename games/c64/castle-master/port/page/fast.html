Castle Master moves you a fixed step each time its main loop goes round. On a C64 a loop takes from a fifth of a second to three seconds, most of it spent drawing the view, so in the Wilderness you walk in steps of 240 of the game's units and turn 15 degrees at a time. A C64 with a much faster processor would go round the loop hundreds of times a second and take a whole step each time. With Fast CPU on, the page plays the game as such a machine would if it were built to stay playable: a new frame 60 times a second, and you move as fast as the Speed sets, whatever is in view.

One loop for each tick of the game's clock

The game keeps time with timer A of the C64's first CIA chip, which interrupts the processor every 16,422 cycles, 59.996 times a second. Each tick of that clock counts down the drain on your strength, the scripts' ten-second timer and the wait for the next lightning. With Fast CPU on, each loop starts on a tick, and never more than one loop on the same tick. The loop's own work takes no time, as on a processor fast enough to finish it long before the next tick.

Sixty frames a second is a rate a real C64 shows: an American C64 redraws its screen just under 60 times a second, and a faster processor would put no more frames than that on its screen. It is also the refresh rate of most displays. Each refresh of your display, the page runs the loops it owes the game for the time since the last refresh, and shows the last loop's frame in the same refresh. It never renders a frame between two loops and never holds a frame back, so the frame on screen is always the game's newest. On a 60 Hz display each frame stays up for one refresh; on a 144 Hz display, for two or three.

A refresh runs at most four loops. A display as slow as 15 Hz still keeps up with the clock, and a refresh that comes late, while the browser was busy elsewhere, starts the game again from where it stopped rather than racing to catch up. Counted in the game's own cycles, the same keys pressed on the same loops make the same game on any display.

The clock keeps absolute time

Everything the game times by its clock runs as on a C64, in absolute time: the music, the drain on strength while a sensor watches, the countdown to the next lightning and the scripts' ten-second timer. So does every wait on the clock inside a loop: a door's animation, the view window opening, the pause after a throw. The next loop starts on the first tick after the wait ends. The title, the menus and loading run at the C64's speed. A minute of play is a minute on either machine.

One wait is different. After a message in the panel, such as WALK SELECTED or THE DOOR OPENS, the loop waits until 50 ticks have passed before it puts the area's name back. On a C64 the slow drawing of the view nearly always used that time up already, so play rarely paused. On a fast processor the wait would freeze play for most of a second after every message. With Fast CPU on, play carries on, and the area's name comes back after the same 50 ticks.

The Speed sets how far each loop takes you

Fast CPU makes many loops where the C64 made one. Anything the game counts in loops would happen that many times as often, so each loop takes only a share of a step. The Speed slider sets how many ticks one C64 step takes, K. Each loop walks, turns or looks 1/K of the C64's step, through the game's own code for a step, and carries the part of a unit left over to the next loop. Holding a key for K ticks moves you exactly one C64 step, whatever is on screen. You travel as fast as a C64 that draws 60/K frames a second, and see 60.

The view between two of the game's angles

A share of a turn leaves you at a fractional angle, between two of the game's 72. The game builds its view at that angle for every job it uses the view for, such as which objects are in sight and what a thrown rock hits: it takes its sines and cosines part of the way to the next angle's, and moves the horizon and the Wilderness's mountains part of the way too. At the game's own angles the screen shows the game's own frame.

The game renders each frame into a buffer off screen and copies it to the screen when it is done, and that copy is when a frame is new. Between two angles, the game's sines and cosines are kept in 64ths, and their rounding makes a wall close to you jump a few pixels from one share to the next. So at a fractional angle the page renders the loop's draw list, the objects and faces the game worked out for the frame, at that angle. With Native resolution on it renders at your screen's full resolution (section 02). Without it, the page renders in the C64's own pixels with a renderer that follows the game's own drawing, pattern by pattern; at the game's own angles that renderer matches the game's frame in 99.16 % of the view's pixels.

You slide along walls that stop a C64 dead

When a step runs into a wall or over an edge, the game cuts the step short and throws the rest away. On a C64 a whole step that meets a wall at an angle stops against it. With Fast CPU each share creeps a little along the wall before it is cut, 60 times a second, so you slide along walls the C64 stops you at, and now and then step off a ledge the C64 keeps you on.
