The game moves the player a fixed step each time its main loop goes round, and on a C64 a round takes from a fifth of a second to three seconds, most of it spent drawing the view. So in the Wilderness the player walks in strides of 240 of the game's units, and turns 15 degrees at a time. Nothing in the loop waits, so a C64 with a much faster processor would go round as fast as it could and take a whole step each time, hundreds a second. With Fast CPU on, the page runs the main loop 60 times a second and gives each round its share of a step, so that a step takes a second whatever is in view, or with Pace at 2× or 5×, half or a fifth of a second.

The interrupt keeps the C64's time

Only the main loop's own work goes faster. Everything the game times by its interrupt runs as before: the music, the drain on strength while a sensor watches, the countdown to the next lightning, and the scripts' timer every ten seconds. So does every wait on the interrupt: a door's animation, the viewport opening, the pause after a shot. A minute of play is a minute on either machine.

Every round lasts one tick of the game's clock

The game's clock is timer A of the C64's first CIA chip, which interrupts the processor every 16,422 cycles, just under 60 times a second. Each tick moves on the scripts' delays, the lightning's countdown, the drain on strength and the scripts' timer. With Fast CPU on, each round of the main loop starts at the same count of that timer, one tick after the last unless the game waited on the interrupt in between. So the same keys, held for the same rounds, make the same game on any computer and at any frame rate.

The page shows the view a round behind, part of the way from one round's picture to the next: the turn and the look turned the shorter way round, and an object that moved between them slid with them. So the view moves at every frame of a screen that shows 120 or 144 a second. What the game changes at once stays sudden: another area, or a jump of more than 1,024 of the game's units. A computer too slow to run 60 rounds a second plays slower than the C64, but its rounds stay one tick long, so the game stays the same.

One pace, whatever is in view

On a C64 the pace of play follows the view. Facing the castle from where the game starts, a round that draws the view takes 1.8 seconds, and the player walks 132 of the game's units a second. Looking up into the sky, a round takes two-thirds of a second; with nothing in view, a fifth. In play, half the rounds take between 0.7 and 1.3 seconds. With Fast CPU on, a step takes a second wherever the player looks: a whole turn takes 24 seconds, and in the Wilderness the player walks 240 units a second. At 2× a step takes half a second, and at 5× a fifth, as fast as a C64 goes facing away from everything. Each pace is a round the C64 itself plays at in some view.

The game counts some things in rounds and others in ticks of its clock, and with Fast CPU on the two keep one ratio at each pace. The player covers the same ground between two of the scripts' ten-second ticks whichever way they face, where on a C64 they cover less facing the castle. The faster paces cover more ground between ticks, as a faster C64 would, so the clocks that wear strength down matter less.

Each round takes a share of a step

Each round walks its share of the C64's stride, a sixtieth of it at 1×, and carries the part of a unit left over to the next round. It turns and looks its share of the C64's 15 degrees the same way. So the player stops as soon as the key comes up, and in open ground a second of walking at 1× covers one of the C64's strides. The scripts that a step or a turn sets off run each round, as on a C64, so up to 60 times a second. On a step they only show or hide objects by the game's flags, which comes out the same however often it is done.

Walls and edges are where the two can part. The game settles a step that meets a wall or leaves an edge at once: it cuts the step short and drops the rest. A C64 stride that meets a wall at an angle stops against it. Each sixtieth creeps a little along the wall before it is cut, so 60 of them a second slide along walls the C64 stops at, and a walk that meets walls or edges can end somewhere the C64's strides do not, now and then on the floor below a ledge the C64 kept the player on.

The view between two of the game's angles

The game keeps the sines and cosines of its 72 angles in 64ths. At those angles each picture is the game's own. Between them the rounding of the 64ths jumps, and a wall close to the player moves a few pixels at once. So with Fast CPU on, the game works out each view at the angle between, for what it uses the view for (which objects are in sight, what a shot hits), and the page paints the picture from the same objects at that angle (section 02). With Native resolution it paints at the screen's own resolution. Without it, the page paints in the C64's pixels with its own painter, which follows the game's: each pattern row by row, the sky and the floor, the mountains, the lightning and the sight. At the game's own pictures that painter matches the game's drawing in 99.16 % of the view's pixels; the rest are at the edges of faces, a pixel either way.
