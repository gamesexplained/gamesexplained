1985 · Mastertronic

Kane

Every figure is built from hardware sprites over a picture: the archer and the Marshall are three sprites stacked, the horse six. Their shapes are packed, and unpacked with each picture and each ride, and a figure turns round by having its shapes mirrored where they lie. The gunmen have no stored shapes at all: the game draws each one into his sprite a row at a time.

01 · Everything that moves

Every figure, as each stage moves it

The game does not move its figures on every frame. Each stage has a loop that waits between passes, so the archer's stage moves about every 3.7 frames and the shoot-out about every 2.8, and a ride moves the horse every fourth frame. Here each figure is drawn over the picture behind it, at the place the game puts it.

The shapes and the pictures are unpacked from the listing's bytes as the How it works tab unpacks them. The canyon and the shoot-out are the game's own loops, and the gunmen beside them the game's own gunman_step ($8810), run on the kit's 6502 in this page; the sight's flicker and the collision register, which the game's interrupt keeps, are kept by the page. The stages' loops are timed by delay loops rather than by the screen, so their rates are the averages measured in the emulator; a ride's four frames are counted by its raster interrupt. The stick is played by a script, which keeps the arrows, bullets and tokens from running out, and the random numbers the game reads from its timers come from the page.

The archer and the Marshall are the same three sprites: a black hat and head, white arms and black legs (marshall_stand, $3843). Walking, every pass of the loop moves each sprite on to the shape below its own, and every seventh pass starts them again from $26, $3B and $2D (walk_animate, $3912). The middle sprite starts again one shape lower every other time, so the arms swing through fourteen shapes while the head and the legs go through seven. The archer's middle sprite is his bow arm, which takes one of four shapes for the arrow's angle, and a fifth as he shoots.

Facing is not a second set of shapes. Each shape has a flag in the top rows of the status lines' screen, which the picture covers ($0400 plus its number), and when a figure turns, flip_shape ($89DE) mirrors each shape it needs in place and flips the flag. The stored shapes face right.

A gunman's sprite shape starts empty. Every fifth pass, gunman_step writes the rows of his figure into its right-hand column from the bottom up, one row more each step, so he rises from behind cover; the other two kinds slide in from the side instead, their eleven rows shifted a bit less each step. He waits seven steps, fires and goes back the way he came. Shot, he does not fall: each step ANDs his rows with noise from the timers and the raster line until nothing is left.

On a ride the horse is six sprites in two rows of three, white and single-colour, and the rider a seventh, multicolour. Every fourth frame the horse takes the next frame from the list for what it is doing, and each frame is six shapes from $10 + 6 × its number. The rider bobs between $CA and $CB every second step. A fall throws him off in an arc, as two sprites.

02 · The sprite shapes

Ninety-six shapes for the stages, two hundred and eight for the rides

Two packed streams hold every stored shape: the stages' at $D000, unpacked with each picture to $4800-$5FFF, and the rides' at $DA50, unpacked to $4400-$77FF when a ride begins.

Point at a shape to see what shows it.

Each shape as it is stored, facing right, in the colour of the first sprite that shows it, over the colour of the stage. The bar under a shape says what shows it; a shape with no bar is never shown.

Both sets live in the video chip's second bank, $4000-$7FFF, so a sprite pointer is the shape's address there over 64. In the canyon and the shoot-out the first kilobyte holds the four shapes the game draws while it runs: the sight, twelve dots in a small ring copied in at $402F by start_play, whose colour the interrupt flips between black and white every frame so it flickers; the arrow, shape 1, whose bottom eight rows duck_loop rewrites from nine pictures at $112B for its angle; and the four gunmen's shapes, 1 to 4. On a ride the same kilobyte is the screen, and its last eight bytes the sprite pointers.

The rider thrown from his horse is three shapes that arrive with the program at $4000, where the stages draw, so the game's first act is to copy them into the processor's stack page, $0101-$01C0. Every ride copies them back to $7300, as shapes $CC-$CE, over the end of the rides' unpacked set. Some of the stages' shapes are never shown: $3C-$3E, after the walking arms; $55-$57, three small arrows, where the arrow the game shows is drawn into shape 1 as it flies; and $5C-$7F, empty but for a few stray bytes.

03 · The loading picture

The picture's colours arrive two seconds before the picture

About 98 seconds into Mastertronic's 1985 cassette, the picture comes up whole: KANE in letters made of bones under a cow's skull, a red sky over brown hills, a warrior with a knife and a man in a black hat with a sheriff's star, firing a pistol, and Mastertronic's name at the foot. It stays up, in silence, for two minutes and 13 seconds while the game loads. For the two seconds before it, the blue screen fills with coloured stripes.

Two frames of the cassette, recorded in VICE and drawn as the video chip draws them: the stripes, then the picture, after the 1.9 seconds they lasted on the recorded load. Skip goes straight to the picture.

The stripes are the picture, half shown. Its colours are loaded first, while the screen is blank, and the moment comes when the C64 is looking at the picture's part of memory but is still drawing text, not a picture. Each character it draws takes its colour from the picture, so the picture's colours show through in the shape of whatever characters that memory happens to hold, which here are mostly lines. Two seconds later the C64 is told to draw a picture, and it appears.

The border's stripes are the loader's. While the tape plays, it moves the border on to the next of the C64's sixteen colours again and again, faster than the screen is drawn, so the border fills with bands of every colour.
