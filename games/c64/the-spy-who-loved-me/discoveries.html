Commodore 64 · 1990 · Domark

The Spy Who Loved Me

What the code shows that the screen does not: a development link left in, signs that show a tenth, and a price that goes down as the damage goes up.

01 · A development leftover

The cable to the other computer

The 458 bytes at the start of the program are not part of the game. They are a small receiver that let another computer send code to the C64 down a cable while the game was being written.

The routine at dev_link waits for one-byte commands on the user port, the C64's parallel connector at the back, with the data on $DD01 and a handshake on two bits of $DD00 (dev_get_byte). The commands receive a block into memory, send a block back, set an address and call it, choose which memory is visible while bytes go to and fro, and send or receive the registers saved when the stub was entered (dev_saved_regs).

The game was started by running the stub. The machine on the disk was saved with BASIC's return address still on its stack, under the stub's call to $0A00, so someone typed SYS 2064, the stub's address in decimal; the bytes where that BASIC line would sit are zeros (dev_gap). When the disk was made, the first instruction of the game, at park_loop, held JMP $0A00: a jump to itself that keeps the processor waiting. The disk's copy was taken by freezing the machine there with a cartridge and setting it to resume three bytes on, at game_start.

02 · A slip of a zero

Signs worth ten times what they say

Shooting an enemy makes a sign float up from it with the points it scored. The points go straight into the score's hundreds (add_score), so an object worth 5 in its table adds 500 to the score, and one worth 10 adds 1,000. The signs, though, are sprite frames that read 50 and 100 (score_popup, sprites). Every sign shows a tenth of what was scored, which a player watching the score climb could see.

Running over a person works the other way and makes no sign: hit_person takes 500 off the score, plays a sound, and stops at zero rather than letting the score go negative. Commodore Format's review said 50 points.6

03 · Arithmetic

Armour that costs less the more it is damaged

The armour replacement has no fixed price: the shop works it out from the damage. A car a point below three-quarters armour pays 1 token, and a car a point above pays 8.

The armour is a byte from 255, full, down to 0 (armour). show_item takes the damage, 255 minus the armour, divides it by 8 to get 0 to 31, keeps the bits $17 of that, and adds 1. The prices are counted in decimal digits, two to a byte, so a byte above 9 in either half would not be a price at all; the mask keeps both halves in range by clearing the bit worth 8. That bit is also the one that is set for a damage of 64 to 127 or 192 to 255, so the price climbs from 1 to 8 tokens over the first quarter of the damage, falls back to 1 and climbs to 8 again over the second, then jumps to 11 and climbs to 18 twice over the second half.

04 · Bought early, given late

Weapons that wait for the sea bed

The miniature lasers, the side rear bolts, and the torpedoes go into stores of their own when they are bought (shop_items), not into the weapons the fire button uses. Only when the sea bed starts does section_3 move them across. On the coast road, where the truck is, they do nothing, and a car that never buys the sub capability has paid for them in vain (the shop). Losing a life before the sea bed throws them away too: after_death empties all three stores before the stage starts again.

05 · Unheard sounds

Two effects nothing plays

The music player holds sixteen tunes, six bytes each in tune_table, and every sound in the game is one of them, started by play_tune. No call starts tune 6 or tune 7, so neither is heard in the game. You can play both on the Music tab.

The player also keeps the third voice for itself only for tunes from 40 up, which a check at $EA82 tests; with sixteen tunes, that never happens. The theme and the effects never sound together: while the theme is on, a call for an effect only starts the theme again once it has ended, and pressing C= swaps the theme for the effects, playing tune 8 to say so (read_keyboard, the music switch).

06 · Saving bytes

Instruments that share a byte

Each of the player's instruments is ten bytes of settings, but they are stored nine bytes apart (set_instrument). The last byte of one instrument is the first byte of the next, so every instrument but the last shares a byte with its neighbour, and the two settings have to agree. The tunes were written around that rule.

07 · A closed door

RESTORE does nothing

On a C64 the RESTORE key goes straight to the processor's non-maskable interrupt, which a game cannot switch off. The game points that interrupt at a single RTI, return from interrupt (nmi_ignore), so pressing the key changes nothing.
