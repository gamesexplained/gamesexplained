Commodore 64 · 1990 · Domark

The Spy Who Loved Me

Bond's Lotus drives, floats, and dives through five scrolling stages, buying its gadgets from Q with tokens picked up on the road.

A car that turns into a submarine

Two submarines, one Russian and one British, have disappeared. Bond and the Soviet agent Anya Amasova follow a microfilm to the shipping magnate Karl Stromberg, and Bond is sent to Sardinia to find out what Stromberg is planning at Atlantis, his fortress under the sea. Q has given him a Lotus that can become a submarine.1

The manual describes six scenes. Three of them, the supertanker, the targeting puzzle, and the corridors of Atlantis with Jaws, are marked "16-bit versions only".1 The C64 keeps the driving: five stages that scroll up the screen, numbered 0 to 4 in the code (start_section). The Lotus leaves the harbour by road and becomes a speedboat where the road meets the water (enter_water). Back on land, a coast road leads to a ramp, and a car with the sub capability takes the ramp into the sea and drives along the sea bed. The last stage is on the open sea, and after it the game starts again at the harbour (section_end).

Along the way the car picks up Q tokens, and on the coast road it drives into the back of Q's truck to spend them in the shop. Seventy-five buy the sub capability; without it the ramp only throws sparks and the coast road goes round again. Collisions and enemy fire wear down the armour, and when it runs out the car crashes and the stage starts again, until the five lives are gone.

The people behind the game

Domark published The Spy Who Loved Me in 1990, its fifth Bond game after A View to a Kill, The Living Daylights, Live and Let Die, and Licence To Kill.1 The game was made by The Kremlin, Domark's own development team, whose logo, a hammer and sickle over an onion-domed church, opens the credits.2 The credits name Neil Harding for the code and Tony West for the graphics; Tony West had also drawn the graphics of Licence To Kill the year before.

The music is by Matt Furniss, whose name is not on the game's screens.23 His player holds sixteen tunes: the James Bond theme, which plays for 95 seconds and repeats, and fifteen short effects. You can hear every one of them on the Music tab.

Domark planned a cartridge version for the C64GS console with all the game's sections, which Neil Harding has confirmed; it was never released.7

"More in common with Spyhunter than the word 'Spy'"

The C64 game cost £9.99 on cassette and £14.99 on disk.4 The reviews of the C64 version ran from fair to poor, and every one of them complained about the scrolling.

Zzap!64 gave the game 42 % for presentation, 63 % for graphics, 39 % for sound, 45 % for hookability, and 36 % for lastability, and found "jerky scrolling, sluggish car movement and a far too laid back game pace".4 When the budget re-release came out, the magazine did not score it again: "Scoring 38% in Issue 69, it's not really worth any more as a budget offering."9 Your Commodore saw the likeness to the arcade game Spy Hunter that the heading quotes, and Commodore Format said the coast road "plays a bit like" it.

What the reviewers did not see

The speed of the scrolling is the player's to choose: the higher the Lotus drives up the screen, the faster the road moves, and every row that scrolls past adds points, so fast driving scores more (steering and speed). The code also keeps things no player was meant to notice. The program still holds the link its author used to send code from another computer, the floating signs show a tenth of the points they stand for, and the price of new armour rises and falls as the damage grows.
