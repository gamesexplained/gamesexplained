1984 · Mastertronic

Squirm

Nothing in Squirm is a sprite. The player, the worms, the eggs and the treasure are characters, and they move by being written into the next cell of the screen. Their animation is the character set itself, rewritten about eight times a second.

01 · Everything that moves

Every moving character, as the game animates it

About 8.6 times a second the game's interrupt rewrites the shapes of a few characters, so every egg rolls and the player spins wherever they are on the screen. In play the player and the worms move a cell at a time, at most a cell a pass, ten passes a second on the first levels.

The characters are drawn from the game's bytes in the built site.

The shapes are the character set at $2800, from the listing's bytes, and they change because the page runs the game's own interrupt, irq_handler ($3CB3), on the kit's 6502 once a jiffy, the tick of the C64's clock, 60 times a second. On every seventh call it rotates the arrow's three frames, swaps each treasure's two poses, rolls the egg and calls rotate_spinner ($2508). The worm and the player move a cell every six jiffies, a pass of the main loop at the first levels' speed (step_delay, $2689); where they go here, the page chooses.

The movement comes from the character set itself. On every seventh call of its interrupt the game rewrites a few characters' shapes: each row of the egg rolls one pixel to the left, each treasure swaps between two poses, and the eight frames of a spinning figure move round one place. Every egg on the screen rolls at once, because every egg is the same character and the game changes its one shape.

The player is character $51, one of eight in a row, $4E-$55, that hold the frames of the spinning figure. rotate_spinner moves every shape down one character, the first going to the end, so $51 shows each frame in turn. The three lives at the bottom of the screen are $4E-$50, other frames of the same figure, and the title's border is made of all eight.

A worm is a head, $43, and a body, $44, written into the cells it crawls along, and nothing about it is animated. The treasure is $47 or $49, the next picture after each one is collected (treasure_char, $268E); each wobbles because the interrupt swaps its shape with the character after it, $48 or $4A, which nothing draws. Nor does anything draw the arrow, $40-$42, though the interrupt turns it all game.

None of these characters has a colour of its own. A cell's colour is set once, as a level starts, from where it is on the screen, so a worm or the player changes colour cell by cell as it moves. How the maze's colours are set is on the How it works tab.

02 · The character set

Every character, and what draws it

The screen is characters from the game's own set at $2800, all 256 of them. The maze uses its walls in multicolour, two wide pixels to a pair of bits; everything else is drawn the ordinary way, a pixel to a bit.

Point at a character to see what draws it.

The characters as the listing holds them, sixteen to a row: the walls in multicolour, blue and blue with white, as the maze shows them, and every other character in white on black.

The set is also the game's alphabet. $01-$1A is an italic A to Z and $30-$39 the digits, so the game's text is ordinary screen codes. The walls take $1B-$25, all below $28, which is how the game tells a wall from anything the player may walk into (try_cell, $2FF9). Fifty-four characters, $5E-$7F, $9B-$9F and $A1-$AF, are never drawn: they hold a table of instruction names left over from the assembler the game was written with. The last seven bytes of $FF are code.
