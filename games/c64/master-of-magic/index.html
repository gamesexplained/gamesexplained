A one-screen dungeon crawl that fits four levels into a 4 KB map, casts 28 rays to decide what you see, lets the video chip find the walls, and settles every spell without rolling a die.

01 · The screen

One frame, rebuilt from memory

The play screen is three screens stacked. An interrupt at $2653 changes the video chip's mode twice on the way down every frame: coloured text for the view and the messages, the same text on an orange ground for the menu, and a multicolour bitmap in another memory bank for the pictures along the bottom.

Drawn by this page from a snapshot of the game in play: the screen at $0400 and its colour memory, the character set at $3000, the bitmap rows at $B400 with their colours at $AA80, and the sprite positions the interrupt copies from $3A00 and $3A20. The yellow dot in the middle of the view window is you.

The view window in the top left is an 11 × 11 opening onto the dungeon map, with your cell in the middle. The status line holds the mind (M) and body (B) bars and a clock. Messages scroll up the yellow window. The menu across the middle lists only what you can do where you stand. Along the bottom, pictures of everything you can see.

02 · Line of sight

28 rays, cast a pixel at a time

The view window shows only what you could see. Each turn the game casts rays from your exact pixel, one pixel step at a time through the drawn walls of the map's characters, and a cell is shown once a ray has reached it.

A port of the ray caster $0B25 and the movement routine $0D44, run on the map and the character set from the game. Walk with the arrow keys or the buttons.

The 28 rays fan out clockwise from north. Each follows a seven-step pattern of single-pixel moves (table $3BF7), and the pauses written into the patterns stop every ray within about 40 pixels, five cells, so the lit area is round. A ray stops at the first wall pixel it meets. Doors are the exception that needs a second rule: a shut door's panel is drawn in the cell's own colour, and the caster stops at that colour only in cells whose colour byte is exactly $0F. Six rays are cast per turn and the window is redrawn every 29.

Walls are found by the video chip

No routine checks the map to stop you walking into rock. You are a sprite, a 4 × 4 dot, and the video chip reports whenever a sprite's pixels overlap anything drawn in the foreground. The interrupt collects that report every frame into $038D; when it shows your dot touching a wall, the next step goes the other way ($0F95). The floor is drawn in a background colour, and water, rock and door panels in foreground colours, so all of them stop you.

03 · The dungeon

Four levels side by side in one map

The whole dungeon is one 4 KB block at $9000: 32 rows of 128 cells, each byte the character the view window shows. The 128 columns are four levels of 32, laid out left to right.

The map as loaded, drawn with the game's own characters as if every cell had been seen. Start places from the tables at $3EF0 and $3F30. The full-size maps are on the Maps tab.

A staircase is two characters. Step onto the right-hand half of $B0 $B1 and $20CC adds 32 to your map address, which puts you on the same row of the next level to the east; step onto the left-hand half of $B2 $B3 and it subtracts 32. There are seven of each, and a single trapdoor, $A3, that drops you one way from level 1 to level 2. You start on level 4. The amulet lies at the end of a winding corridor on the east edge of level 1, a few cells from the minotaur, and the pedestal it has to go back to is beside the small pool on level 4.

Doors come in pairs of cells, one each side of the wall line. Bit 0 of the character says open or shut, and two more bits say where the other half is, so OPEN and CLOSE can flip both. There are 17 doors, nine on level 1, seven on level 3, one on level 4 and none on level 2, and a new game shuts them all.

04 · Choosing what to do

A menu of what applies, and a clock that counts turns

Every time you choose, $11A2 builds the menu again from what is around you, and the clock stops until you have chosen.

The menu builder's rules, in its own order, drawn with the game's characters. Each option costs a fixed number of world passes (table $4C27), shown beside it.

What happens between choices is measured in passes of $08CC: each pass casts six rays, gives every creature six ticks of its clock and advances the game clock by an eighth of a second. The menu waits for you with no passes at all, which is why the clock stands still there. A choice then buys a fixed number of passes, whether or not the action did anything: INVENTORY costs 12, CAST 20, SWAP 3. RUN keeps passing until you press fire, at about 1.2 cells a second.

There are 18 verbs in the table and the menu can only ever show 17. WALK, which moves you at half the speed of RUN, has a string and a handler at $08B0, and the builder never adds it.

05 · The cast

Thirty pictures for 64 things

Everything you can see appears in the strip at the bottom as a picture, cut from one multicolour bitmap sheet at $6000. Most kinds share one picture, and seven are drawn twice as tall.

Pictures cut from the sheet the way $1515 copies them into the strip. Numbers from the creature tables; "chases" is how many decisions a creature keeps hunting you after losing sight of you. Doors and staircases have pictures too: the strip shows one for every door or staircase character in the view window.

A creature whose body strength goes below zero is marked dead by bit 7 of that byte, and the strip lays a sprite over its picture that spells DEAD, in light red, doubled in size. There are eight such sprites, all pointing at the same shape at $A500.

06 · Fighting

A roll of 0 to 31 against a defence

Every blow is a random number from 0 to 31 plus the attacker's to-hit score, against the defender's defence. Damage is the random number masked by the weapon plus an eighth of the attacker's strength.

Chances counted over the actual random numbers. The generator at $2B9E reads three pages of the KERNAL ROM and repeats every 256 numbers, and the numbers in one cycle are far from even: 160 of the 256 possible values turn up.

Two vampires, one table off by two

Strength, defence and to-hit are three tables of 64 entries, indexed by entry number. They were placed only 62 bytes apart, so the last two entries of each, the second vampire and the minotaur, are the first two of the next. The second vampire's defence is the player's to-hit score, 25, where the first vampire's is 60, and the minotaur's defence is to-hit entry 1, an unused object slot that holds 133.

With a defence of 133, no roll reaches the minotaur, and the Dagger of Death is the only way through: $2B93 replaces the roll with the minotaur's own defence, a certain hit, and $2B26 makes the damage 30. The first vampire, at 60, is out of reach of a roll that tops out at 56, unless you wear the Ring of Dexterity, which adds 8.

Bonuses that can never apply

The routine that adjusts a roll for special weapons has two tests that cannot pass. Each asks whether one register equals two different values in turn:

; $2B60: the weapon ($03A8) is a mace, and the target a skeleton or hellhound? lda $03A8 cmp #$16 ; mace 1? bne $2B78 ; if not, skip cmp #$17 ; ...and mace 2? A is still $16, so no bne $2B78 ; always taken: the +8 below is never reached cpx #$28 ... adc #$08 ; $2B7B: the dagger of wood against either vampire cmp #$05 bne $2B8C cpx #$3D ; target vampire 1? bne $2B8C ; if not, skip cpx #$3E ; ...and vampire 2? X is still $3D, so no bne $2B8C lda $4E04 ; the roll = vampire 1's defence: a certain hit

The protection routine $24F1 has the same shape for the shield, three compares in a row against $1E, $1F and $20, so a shield never protects. Its other tests read the wrong byte: they check the object's "can be worn" flag, which is always set for armour, helmet and rings, where the "worn" state was surely meant. So armour protects you while you only hold it in a hand, and the ring with the blue stone, the one the scrolls call cursed, makes monsters hit more often whenever you hold it.

07 · Magic

Spells that never miss by chance

The instructions promise that Magic Missile has a high chance of missing. The code fetches a random number for it and throws it away.

; $3AD3 spell_hit_creature jsr $2B9E ; a random number into A lda #$1F ; ...overwritten with 31 clc adc $57D4 ; + the spell's power ldx $03A7 cmp $4DC7,x ; against the target's defence bpl hit

MAGIC MISSILE has power 20, FIREBALL and ENERGY DRAIN 60. Tested in the emulator: casting from six different random states gave the same result every time.

So a spell either always hits a creature or always misses it. MAGIC MISSILE always hits everything but the first vampire and the minotaur, which it always misses; FIREBALL and ENERGY DRAIN miss only the minotaur. The cost in mind is paid before you pick a target, so choosing -NOTHING- still spends it. The Magical Shield takes 8 off every creature's roll and a point of mind every 100 passes, and while it is up the menu offers UNCAST instead of CAST, so no other spell can be cast.

08 · The monsters

Why the monsters drift north

The instructions say most monsters patrol round their lair. The code gives every creature a home to walk back to when it is not hunting you, and the new-game routine wipes half of each address.

A port of the creature clock $2205, the decisions $2308 and the move chooser $2566, checked pass by pass against the game running in the emulator.

The home addresses live in two 31-byte tables at $4CAE and $4CCD. The set-up at $0924 clears two flag tables next to them with a loop that runs for 64 entries, so the first store runs on through every home's high byte. A home of $00ll is a page-0 address above the top of the map, and an idle creature heads for it: first towards its start column, then due north, until it presses against the northernmost wall it can reach. The file was saved after the game had already run once, so the loaded homes are already wiped. Seven creatures ignore their homes and get a random destination every 50 passes.

A creature that sees you re-arms a pursuit counter: bats give up at once, orcs after 6 decisions, skeletons 20, vampires 30, the wizard and the minotaur 60. Chasing, it walks to where it last saw you and carries straight on. Orcs, skeletons, vampires, the wizard and the minotaur open a shut door in their way and close it behind them; hellhounds, bats, spiders and snakes treat it as a wall. One snake is timid: it runs from you and never attacks.

09 · The demonstration

A recording of someone playing, and the recorder that made it

After one pass of the title's story the game plays itself. It does it by replaying a recording of joystick input from $A543, with the random numbers reset to where they were when it was recorded.

The recording, one byte per run of identical input: the stick in the high four bits, the number of polls less one in the low four. . It ends with an idle stretch and the end mark the recorder writes when its buffer runs out.

The recorder is still in the game. Hold CTRL and press R on the title screen, and $5BBA saves the random state and starts a game in which every input is written over the demonstration; F ends it. The next time the title's story runs out, the demonstration plays your recording.

10 · The sound

Rob Hubbard's three tunes, from the stored bytes

The music driver at $C000 plays three tunes: the main theme on the title and in play, a short one when you die and a longer one when you win.

A port of the driver's reading of its patterns and instruments, checked frame by frame against the original code, feeding a small model of the sound chip.

The main theme ends in a fade: the driver sets the volume from how far voice 3 has got through its list of patterns, so the last minute of the five-and-a-half-minute theme fades to silence before it loops. Press M in play and the music stops. Then you hear something else: every third pixel you move, and every third step of most monsters in sight, the interrupt plays a short burst of noise, a footstep. With the music on, the steps are silent.

11 · The title picture

A picture hidden under the KERNAL

The title picture sits at $E000, in the RAM underneath the KERNAL ROM. The video chip always reads RAM, so it shows the picture; the processor at the same addresses reads the ROM.

Rebuilt from the bitmap at $E000, the screen matrix at $CC00 and the colours copied from $AC00. The story scrolls along the bottom in ordinary text, two pixels a frame.

The game uses both views of those addresses. The random number routine reads three 256-byte pages at $E000, $E800 and $F000. It gets KERNAL code, because the KERNAL is switched in, and uses it as noise. The picture's bytes at the same addresses are never read by the processor.

12 · Secrets, quirks and bugs

Things the manual never told you

Secret: the edge of a pool is a door live

The menu decides whether you stand on a door with a signed compare ($11E5: CMP #$F0, BMI), so any character below $70 passes too. On level 2 the top edge of the big pool is two such cells, floor except for a line of water, and standing on one offers OPEN. Choosing it prints "OPEN DOOR." and spends eight passes; the door routine checks for real door characters and changes nothing.

A reset leaves BASIC switched in live

The game catches RESTORE and the reset button with the CBM80 signature at $8004, and both restart it. After a reset, though, the processor's data direction register is zero, and the restart never sets it again, so switching BASIC out has no effect. The game's tables at $A000 read as BASIC ROM: the title comes up in the wrong colours and the view window stays empty.

The first game after loading rolls different dice

The random numbers depend on two indices that step by +1 and −3, so the sequence repeats every 256 numbers, on one of 256 separate cycles. The demonstration resets them to its own starting point, and every game after it continues on that cycle. A game started straight after loading, before the demonstration has ever run, starts from the indices saved in the file and runs on a different cycle, with different odds: the wizard is hit 45 % of the time instead of 47 %.

Two descriptions nobody can read

EXAMINE picks one of 28 descriptions through a table at $571E. No entry points at text 3, "THE WRITING ON IT SAYS -THE SAPPHIRE IS CURSED, IT BRINGS BAD LUCK-", or at text 5, "IT HAS A SHARP EDGE AND A LONG BLADE". The warning about the cursed ring is in the game and can never be shown.

The wizard who will heal your wounds

A scroll says "THE KIND OLD WIZARD LEGGOLESS WILL HEAL YOUR WOUNDS", and EXAMINE calls him "A SHORT HARMLESS LOOKING WIZARD". Nothing in the game raises your body strength except the two healing potions, and the wizard's FIREBALL SPELL does 3 to 18 damage, the hardest blow of any creature. Two other scrolls say the Dagger of Death "WEAKENS HIS MIND"; in the code it takes 30 from the minotaur's body.

A timed action cannot be cut short live

After every action the pass loop tests the fire button, as if pressing it could end the action early. It can only end RUN: nothing reads the joystick during the other actions' passes, so the test never sees fire. INVENTORY took its 12 passes with fire held all the way through.

A machine-code monitor left in the middle

Between the menu verbs and the nouns, $41D9–$4587 holds 943 bytes of a machine-code monitor, the kind with commands for memory, assembly and disk: nothing calls it, and its own jumps land in the game's tables. Its remains also fill the unused object slots of several creature tables, among them its disassembler's table of addressing modes at $4F06.
