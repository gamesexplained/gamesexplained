The whole game, ported routine by routine from its 6502 code to JavaScript and run in step with the original. It starts as a C64 does once the game has loaded: the title, the story of Thelric and the amulet, and then the game's own demonstration.

Play with W, A, S, D and the mouse. Click a word in the menu to pick it, and click anywhere else on the picture to bring the menu back. The mouse is the page's own addition: a click on a word moves the black cursor to it with the joystick, as a player would, and presses fire, and a click elsewhere is fire, which stops a RUN. W, A, S and D or the arrow keys are the joystick in port 2 and Enter is its fire button; the other keys of a PC keyboard stand where the C64's do. RUN walks until fire is pressed; every other verb takes its own time and then the menu comes back. On a touch screen the pad is the joystick.

01 · The port

What runs here

Nothing on this page emulates a 6502. The game is written out in JavaScript, one function for each routine of the listing, from its first instruction at $07F9: the title $5B27 with its picture and story scroller, the page of controls and the five pages of instructions, the main menu $11A2 and its chooser $1320, every verb, the line of sight $0B25, the player's movement $0D44, the creatures with their pursuit and attacks $2205, the four spells, the potions, the picture strip $1515, the clock and the bars, the death, the win and the wait after them $0876. The demonstration is the game's own recorder and player $179D, which replays its recorded controls wherever the game reads the joystick, and records yours after CTRL and R.

The interrupts are ported too. On the title one handler $5D08 splits the screen between the picture and the story's line, moving the line two pixels a frame; in play another $2653 splits it three ways, the view with its sprites, the message window and menu, and the picture strip, and plays the music or, with the music off, the footsteps. The music driver at $C000 runs on the game's own memory, as the interrupt calls it. The game calls a few routines of the C64's ROMs (the screen clear, CHROUT for the instructions and the keyboard scan); they are written out here too.

The port runs on the game's own 64 KB, loaded from the bytes the Source code tab shows, and keeps every variable where the game keeps it: the objects' and creatures' tables at $3D45, the map at $9000, the demonstration's recording at $A540 and the operands the game rewrites in its own code. The screen is drawn by the site's model of the video chip from that memory, with each write to the chip at the line and cycle where the page's clock stood, and the sound is the port's writes to the SID, played through the site's model of it.

02 · The test

Every pass checked against the game's own code

test_play.js runs the port beside the game's own code on the kit's model of the C64, from the first instruction on, and compares the two at the start of every pass of the world $08CC: all of memory, colour memory, every register written to the video chip and the SID, and CIA 1's ports. Its interrupts run in both, from the same moments. The model plays the game's demonstration as VICE does, pass for pass, and in the run the two stay identical through 4,458 passes: the title and the whole story, the demonstration to its end and the wait after it, a player who reads the instructions, picks every verb the menus offer, runs about with the stick and the keys, turns the music off and on, wins and dies, and a game recorded with CTRL and R, ended with F and played back by the title.

Two bytes are left out of the comparison: $0385 and $0386, where the random number routine saves its caller's X and Y registers. The port keeps no registers, so it has no values to put there, and nothing reads them back but the routine's own exit.

Walls are found by the video chip. Each frame the play interrupt copies its sprite-to-background collision register $D01F into $038D, and the game reads it before each step. The site's model of the collisions, collide.js, works them out as the beam draws the sprites and the graphics, on the kit's machine and on this page alike. The page keeps the C64's pace by keeping a clock: each piece of work the port does moves it on by what the game's own code took for it on the kit's machine, the interrupts run on their raster lines and on CIA 1's timer, and the video chip takes its cycles from the processor on each bad line and each sprite's fetch. Run on that clock alone, the page plays the game's demonstration as the game's code does, every one of its 2,371 passes, beginning in the frame the kit's machine begins it and ending 30 of its 14,119 frames sooner, 0.21 % fast.

The screen is drawn by the site's model of the video chip from the port's memory, with each change to memory where the game's code made it. On the title the picture half of the interrupt, at line $30, shows video bank 3 and moves the story's line a character on in bank 0, which the scroller half, at line $EE, shows with the fine scroll it writes. test_screen.js draws the title sixty times a second and holds the story to a move of 0 or 2 pixels left from one drawing to the next.

03 · Not ported

What is left out

  • The RESTORE key and the reset button, which on a C64 restart the game at its title through the cartridge vectors it leaves at $8000.
  • An NTSC machine. The game slows its music and its story scroller to keep their pace there; the page is a PAL C64.
  • The ROM routines beyond what the game uses of them: the screen clear, the characters CHROUT prints for the instructions, and of the keyboard scan the key held in $C5 and the SHIFT, Commodore and CTRL keys in $028D, the only results the game reads.
