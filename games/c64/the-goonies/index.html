Datasoft turns the film’s treasure hunt into a game of cooperation. Two Goonies share each screen, and getting them both out means discovering how one can clear a path for the other.

Working together to escape

The Goonies are a gang of children following a pirate’s treasure map. Their journey takes them from the Fratelli gang’s hideout through underground caverns to One-Eyed Willy’s ship. The game divides that journey into eight single-screen puzzles.1

The first screen establishes the idea. One Goonie operates a money press, distracting Mama Fratelli with falling banknotes. The other tips a water tank to extinguish a fire, opening the way down through the fireplace. Both must reach the exit before the next scene begins.

Playing alone, you press fire to switch characters, leaving one in position while moving the other. With two players, each has a joystick and both can move at once. The same puzzle becomes a task you coordinate with another person.

Later scenes give that cooperation different forms. The Goonies close vents to burst a pipe, direct rolling rocks onto a lever, and pile eggs into a staircase. Each screen asks you to understand its machinery as well as negotiate its platforms.

The people behind the game

The credits recorded by C64-Wiki name Scott Spanburg as programmer, Kelly Day as graphics artist, and John A. Fitzpatrick as musician. Their names do not appear in the game’s ordinary screens.2

Two traces survive inside the program. Pressing V displays “V 1 BY SES”. The initials probably refer to Spanburg, though that identification remains an inference. Fitzpatrick’s name appears in full in the copyright text of his Pearl Music Player, the software that plays the game’s tunes.

Day’s graphics give each location a different arrangement within a restricted palette. The hideout’s stacked floors eventually give way to the pirate ship’s decks and rigging. The character artwork changes between scenes too: the journey uses different pairs drawn from seven children’s animation sets.

A welcome idea in four colours

In Britain, U.S. Gold released The Goonies at £9.95 on cassette and £14.95 on disk. The ZZap!64 review in its January 1986 issue found the puzzles more appealing than the presentation.3

The panel praised the problems set on each screen. Its assessment of the controls was direct: “Novel control method is fun.” The graphics received a much colder response, with one reviewer complaining of “a distinct lack of colour, with only four used!” The overall score was 67%. Read the review transcription at GameBase64.

Those four colours run through the playing scenes: black, red, blue, and white. The game draws moving characters directly into its background pictures. The Graphics page shows the shapes and explains how the picture is restored as they move.

The edition matters here. These pages examine a Datasoft disk; the British review concerns the U.S. Gold release. A comparison of the two disks found different program builds and music data, so the tunes available here belong specifically to the Datasoft copy.

What survives beneath the adventure

The program contains a small piece of the makers’ working material: assembler source text for the cage scene. Its original table names describe doors and levers, and the fragment matches the corresponding data and instructions in the finished game. The Discoveries page puts that surviving source beside its explanation.

Reaching the treasure also reveals something about the adventure’s structure. After the ship sails away, the game returns to the hideout with your score and remaining lives intact. Another journey begins, and another ending can pay its bonus.
