01 · The account number

The saved game is an eight-digit number that holds your money and a check on your name

A game that ends with more money than it started with prints a new account number. Typed back in at the start, with the same name, it gives the player that money to spend. There is no disk file: the number is the save.

The account number the game prints

The game's routines ported: $90EE makes the number, $9155 checks it, $9CFC reads what was typed, $91B6 adds up the name and $91A6 steps the check. Run on 20,000 random names, balances and typed numbers beside the game's own code, every result matched.

The number carries the balance in hundreds of dollars, four decimal digits kept in two bytes ($57, $58); the tens and units are dropped. A third byte is a check worked out from the name: the balance's two bytes are added, and the sum is stepped through an eight-bit shift register once for every unit of the name's byte total. The 24 bits are written as eight octal digits, and the game prints them in pairs with each pair reversed.

What the check lets through

  • An eight-bit check lets one number in 256 through for any name. Typing numbers at random finds a working one in a few hundred tries.
  • Only the sum of the name's bytes counts, so names with the same letters share every account: STANTZ,RAY and TZANTS,RAY.
  • The reader keeps three bits of each typed character. An 8 reads as 0 and a 9 as 1, so 64485184 is the same account as 64405104. Letters get through too, by their character code: LEFTY typed as the number, with the name LEFTY, gives $860,000.
  • Nothing checks that the decoded balance is a decimal number. A blank name with 458 decodes to hexadecimal $A0 in the top byte and the vehicle page shows YOU HAVE $:00000. Discoveries says what that does to the end of the game.

03 · The drive

The drive's length is the route's dots plus five

Choosing a building sets the car off along the dotted route drawn on the map. The drive scene is an overhead road; Roamers met on the map come back on it, where the vacuum can catch them.

A frame of the drive, drawn from memory, recorded in the kit's simulator. The road scrolls by the fine scroll register ($7024) and a whole-character shift every eight pixels ($34F9).

The map counts the dots of the route as the logo moves ($7F29). A Roamer the logo touches on the way is marked with the dot count at that moment ($7DAA) and meets the car at that distance on the road, two at a time at most ($8117). The car accelerates by one a frame to its top speed and brakes at the end.

The ghost vacuum needs a fresh press of fire with the Roamer within 32 pixels behind the car or 15 ahead. It takes 32 frames to pull the Roamer in, and then the Roamer starts again from its place on the map. It earns nothing and changes no PK energy, but it keeps that Roamer from reaching Zuul for a while.

04 · The bust

Crossing the streams costs two men

At the building, one man lays the trap, two men walk into place, fire turns the proton streams on, and the joystick closes the gap between them to herd the Slimer over the trap. Then fire springs it.

The streams on, drawn from memory (simulator).

A building with the trap down (simulator).

The crossing test at $85D4-$8615: with the men facing each other, the streams cross when the gap is below $2A minus a quarter of the height difference. Positions are stored halved ($A0-$AF).

With the streams on and the men 12 or more apart, the stick only moves them towards each other ($85B9, with made-up joystick readings at auto_joystick, $B3AD). The backpacks lose one percent every 16 frames; at zero the trap springs by itself. The trap catches the Slimer if it is within 8 pixels to the left, 7 to the right, 12 above and 3 below the trap's light before the light has risen to $9C.

05 · Controls

The joystick goes in port 1

Most C64 games read the joystick in control port 2, which the keyboard does not share. Ghostbusters reads port 1 and nothing else, and its own keyboard scan gives up while either port reads anything but "nothing pressed" ($96CA, $96E8).

06 · The screens

One raster interrupt a frame runs everything but the game's logic

The title, drawn from memory. All 104,448 pixels match the emulator. The lyric window's smooth scroll is a raster split ($8EB7).

The game draws everything in video bank 1 ($4000-$7FFF): the screen at $5400, the character set at $5800, sixty sprite shapes from $4000. None of it is in the file as it appears: the start-up clears the bank and rebuilds it, unpacking the sprites from a run-length stream ($B408-$BFF7, a zero byte count after each $11), mirroring eight of them for walking left, and copying the upper-case letters from the character ROM with the Activision logo and a few symbols over the top.

The raster interrupt fires at line $B8. In play it steps the random number, counts the tick, scrolls the bottom line, waits for lines $DF and $EC to colour the status band, writes all eight sprites from the positions the game keeps halved, sets the frame flag the main loop waits for, and runs the music and the sound effects. The main loop ($6FEC) does one pass of the game each time the flag is set, ending with the handler for the current state from a table of 64 ($381A).
