Commodore 64 · 1991 · Ocean Software

Toki

Ocean's conversion of TAD Corporation's 1989 arcade platformer, in which a warrior turned into an ape spits fireballs to rescue his captured love. On the C64 it shipped as a 128 KB cartridge, and the game treats that cartridge as a library it copies from rather than a program it runs.

01 · The cartridge

Sixteen banks behind one window

The cartridge holds 128 KB in sixteen 8 KB banks, and the C64 sees one bank at a time at $8000. Switching on starts bank 0, and by the time the game's first routine in RAM runs $51FB, most of the program has been copied out of the cartridge. During play the game keeps the cartridge out of sight and runs entirely from RAM, including a copy of bank 3 at $8000.

When it needs something from the cartridge, a short routine in the stack page brings bank 0 back, looks the item up in a directory of eight-byte entries, switches to the bank that holds it, and copies it into place $011F. The stage names, GAME OVER, and STAGE CLEAR never reach RAM: they stay in bank 0.

Each directory entry names a bank, where to read in it, where to write in RAM, and how many bytes, and one flag bit decides whether the copy writes into the I/O chips or into the RAM beneath them $0153. Only one entry sets that bit: 120 bytes of colours for colour RAM at $DB48. Switching on copies the first six entries $0100, which lay banks 1 to 4 end to end from $4000 to $BD6B and put two pieces of bank 0 low in memory, then jumps into the copy of bank 3 at $8000 $0118. The last fifteen entries come in five sets of three, each filling $C000, $BD76, and $0C01, one set for each of the five stages.

Starting a stage looks up where its set begins in a five-byte table in bank 0, one byte per stage $0B08, and copies entries from there until it reaches one marked as packed $0A9B. The second flag bit marks that packed entry, 2.2 to 3.3 KB copied to $0C01, and the copy hands it to an unpacker in bank 0 $8DA2. The unpacker works backwards from the end of the data and expands each stage in place to between 5.3 and 7.1 KB, filling $0C01 upward. None of a stage's block is code: the routines that run each of the game's objects read from tables in it, so each stage carries its own data for them $A23D. The same unpacker runs at power-on, where four entries from three banks make one packed block that grows to 14.5 KB at $0C01 $5240. Each stage overwrites that block. Two more entries follow, the same for every stage $0B11. The stage number that picks the set starts at 0 for a new game and goes up by one as each stage ends $81B1.

02 · Timing

A frame driven by an NMI

A CIA timer set to one PAL frame raises an NMI every frame $4D1D, and the NMI sets up a raster interrupt for the top of the status panel $837E. That interrupt picks a display mode the VIC-II shows as black, and behind those eight black lines it switches to the panel's own video bank, character set, and colours. A second interrupt turns the black mode off, and the panel appears $BCE1. The main loop runs the game's updates once a frame $80F9 and spends its spare time waiting for the next one $819B.

04 · The frame

Ten updates, in a fixed order

Each frame the main loop calls ten routines one after another $80F9. The player moves after the objects, the view window is worked out from the player's new position, and the sprite tables are filled just before the background strip for the next scroll step is drawn.

Scrolling follows a zone

Every stage is cut into sections, and each section has a record that says how the view behaves there $92DF. In one setting the view follows the player: while he stays inside a zone the view holds still, and when he leaves it the view scrolls two pixels a frame toward that side $470C. Two other settings in the record make the view move by itself, horizontally or vertically, until it meets the section's limit, and then follow the player along the other axis.

A stage skip behind two joysticks

The player routine also tests for a joystick combination that no player would press by accident $8572. The Discoveries tab says what it does.

05 · The release

An arcade game on a cartridge, and the only one Ocean made for 8-bit

TAD Corporation made Toki for the arcades in 1989.1 Ocean Software published the Commodore 64 version in 1991, on a cartridge only, alongside versions for the Amiga and Atari ST.1 The C64 version was the only 8-bit computer version Ocean released, and it leaves out the arcade's full intro, one of its music tracks, and one of its levels.3 The cartridge itself stores five stage names.

The manual credits the program to Al Dukes, the graphics to Mike Clayton, Joff Scarcliffe, and William Harbison, and the music and effects to Keith Tinman.2

Zzap!64: "a fun little conversion"

Zzap!64 reviewed the cartridge in issue 80, January 1992, at £14.99.4 Stuart Wynne and Phil King gave it 82 %: presentation 70 %, graphics 79 %, sound 74 %, hookability 83 %, and lastability 81 %. Wynne's complaint was "Turricanitis": the game offers little over Turrican and cannot compare in size or graphics, and he found the main sprite "rather splodgy". King agreed the graphics were a little blurred, with the odd glitch on big villains, and still found it playable, because the arcade layout switches from horizontal to vertical scrolling and mixes in swimming sections, so "you never know what's at the end of the next screen". Both liked that the cartridge needs no multiload.

Commodore Force: "a fair and lasting romp"

Commodore Force covered the game in the Round-Up of issue 5, May 1993, and gave it a Force Factor of 79 %.5 The reviewer saw another macho hunk turned into a primate, called the graphics "a little basic", and praised the many and varied villains and the levels that must be tackled in different ways, some scrolling vertically and others horizontally. The scan ends mid-sentence, at "a fair and lasting romp is on offer for fans of the".

Commodore Power: "probably worth a go for most people"

Commodore Power reviewed the cartridge at £14.99, with Richard Taylor writing, and gave it a Danger Rating of 73 %: graphics 7, sonics 6, half-life 8, and fix factor 7, each out of 10.6 Taylor liked the control, which lets the player move in mid-jump, and the five levels, each quite large and needing no loading because the game is on a cartridge. His complaint was that once the levels have been sussed they become quite easy, because everything is in the same place. The graphics and sound "aren't outstanding", but it all works well together, and with the new cartridge price the game is "probably worth a go for most people".

06 · The makers

The people behind the cartridge

Al Dukes wrote the program, Mike Clayton, Joff Scarcliffe, and William Harbison drew the graphics, and Keith Tinman made the music and effects.2 The fan remaster's credits give the original team the same way, with Harbison as the backgrounds artist.8 Games That Weren't, a site that records unreleased games, calls the result "a superb conversion by Al Dukes".7

That site had first listed an unreleased earlier version of the C64 game, because the Ocean History book said that Harbison had worked on a conversion with John Meegan that was cancelled. It then asked both men, and found that no earlier edition was in production and that Harbison's graphics were used in the final release.7

The Amiga and Atari ST versions came from a different team. Philippe Dessoly, who was working at Ocean France when he discovered Toki, says he was responsible for them.10 His colleague Pierre Adane recalls that "we were lucky enough to have the arcade cabinet at Ocean Software and we were playing the game every chance we had during the development of the 16-bit versions".10 Ocean also advertised Spectrum and Amstrad CPC versions that never appeared, and CRASH previewed the Spectrum one in issue 90.1

07 · The arcade original

A hard arcade game that critics liked

TAD Corporation released Toki in Japan on 8 December 1989 as JuJu Densetsu, and Fabtek released it in North America in February 1990.1 Wikipedia's text names Akira Sakuma as the designer, and its infobox lists Haruki Kitahara as designer, Sakuma as programmer, and Yukihiko Kitahara as composer.1 The player is a warrior turned into an ape, who spits energy balls at the monsters of a wizard, and almost any attack can kill him.1

In January 1990 Play Meter's survey ranked it second among arcade games in North America.1 Computer and Video Games thought it difficult but fun and addictive. Kurt Kalata of Hardcore Gaming 101 wrote that "there were plenty of prehistoric themed sidescrollers in the late 80s/early 90s", but "none are as quite as delightfully deranged as Toki".1

08 · Afterwards

A fan remaster is redrawing this very cartridge

Sega's Mega Drive and Genesis version, Toki: Going Ape Spit, has extra levels and no power-ups other than the spit upgrades.1 The NES version gives Toki a health bar so that one hit does not kill him, and four Famitsu reviewers still called it a "memorization game" because of its difficulty. The Lynx version was received favourably.1 Magic Team released the arcade game for iOS in 2009, and Microids published a remake in 2018 for the Nintendo Switch, followed by Windows, PlayStation 4, Xbox One, and Luna versions.1 Nintendo World Report gave the Switch remake 7.5 out of 10, praising its new difficulty settings and objecting to "cheap deaths".11

In 2023 Davide Bottino, a pixel artist and journalist for Zzap! Italia, announced on the Lemon64 forums that he would remaster this C64 conversion.9 He called the original "a decent and above average conversion" that left "a lot to be desired", with "crude" sprites for the hero and the bosses.9 The remaster redraws the graphics, with new music by Aldo Chiummo. Indie Retro News reported that it would also bring new sound effects, bug fixes, and better controls.8,9 The project credits reverse engineering, coding, and tools to Mariano Cigliano, Pasquale Sannino, Euan Gamble, Martin Piper, Knight Rider, and Oziphantom.8

Bottino gives the conversion's limits as "only 91 unique 2x2 tiles and a 256-character map".8 The first half agrees with this page's own count: the block table of stage 1 holds 91 blocks, each two characters wide and two high. The second half has not been checked here.

09 · Difficulty

Hard in the arcade, easier once the levels are known on the C64

The sources agree on the arcade game. Computer and Video Games called it difficult, the Famitsu reviewers called the NES version a memorisation game, and the 2018 remake needed difficulty settings and still kills the player in one hit.1,11 The C64 cartridge reads differently. Zzap!64 found the levels fairly big and not hard early on,4 and Commodore Power wrote that once the levels have been sussed they become quite easy, because everything is in the same place.6 The sources read do not say how many players have finished the C64 game.
