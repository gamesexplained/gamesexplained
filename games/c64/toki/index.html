Commodore 64 · 1991 · Ocean Software

Toki

A warrior turned into an ape sets out to rescue his captured love, spitting fireballs at the creatures in his way. Ocean’s cartridge conversion brings Toki’s changing scenery and swimming sections to the Commodore 64, with five stages ready to play without waiting for a tape or disk.

An ape with a rescue mission

Toki began as a 1989 arcade game from TAD Corporation. Its transformed hero fights through a world where the route can turn upwards, descend into water, or carry him across platforms above fire. Jumping and shooting get him through, while collected weapons temporarily change his firepower.1

The C64 version’s five stages lead through Labyrinth of Caves, Lake Neptune, Caverns of Fire, Ice Palace, and Dark Jungle. Each starts with a time limit of four minutes and thirty seconds. Collecting items earns extra lives, and an American football helmet offers a brief spell of invincibility.4

The stage introduction asks you to choose between music and sound effects. Selecting music silences the effects. Hear the music and effects separately.

The people behind the conversion

The C64 manual’s credits, as recorded in the source notes, name Al Dukes as programmer. Mike Clayton, Joff Scarcliffe, and William Harbison supplied the graphics, with music and effects by Keith Tinman. Later credits for the fan remaster also recognise Dukes, Harbison, and Tinman’s original work.2

Harbison’s contribution became tangled in an account of a cancelled conversion. The Ocean History book described an earlier version involving Harbison and John Meegan. Games That Weren’t, the archive of unreleased games, investigated that account and contacted both men. Its conclusion was that there had been no separate earlier C64 version: Harbison’s graphics were used in the released game.7

Ocean’s Amiga and Atari ST conversions came from a different team. In a later interview, programmer Pierre Adane remembered having the arcade cabinet at Ocean and playing it whenever the team had a chance during development. That recollection belongs to the 16-bit conversions, but gives a glimpse of the arcade game inside Ocean’s offices.10

What £14.99 bought

Reviewing the cartridge in the January 1992 issue of Zzap!64, Stuart Wynne and Phil King gave Toki 82 per cent. Both appreciated being able to play without repeated loading. Their reservations concerned what appeared on screen.4

Wynne found the main sprite indistinct and thought the conversion suffered beside Turrican, another C64 action platformer with a larger world and stronger graphics. King also noticed blurred graphics and occasional glitches on large enemies, but liked the changing route. Horizontal and vertical scrolling, together with swimming sections, made the next screen difficult to predict.4

Richard Taylor’s Commodore Power review praised the ability to adjust Toki’s movement during a jump. He also liked the size of the five stages, although their fixed arrangements made them easier once learned. His 73 per cent verdict described a game whose graphics and sound were ordinary, but whose parts worked well together.6

A later Commodore Force round-up awarded 79 per cent. Its reviewer again singled out the varied enemies and stages that demanded different approaches. Across the reviews, the strongest case for Toki was the changing journey and the control of its hero.5

How the cartridge feeds the game

The absence of loading waits was part of the cartridge’s appeal. Inside, its extra storage supplies the game a piece at a time.

The cartridge holds 128 KB, twice the C64’s main memory. At startup, Toki copies its program into the computer’s RAM and runs from there. Starting a stage brings in another set of graphics and data, including a compressed block that expands in memory. The same program can then run a different landscape and its objects. $011F $0AFF

That division gives each stage its own material without requiring five separate copies of the engine. The Maps / levels page shows backgrounds reconstructed from those stage records.

There is also a stage skip hidden behind an unlikely combination of two joysticks. Holding the combination enables the Commodore key to advance the stage; the first-to-second-stage transition has been tested. See the combination and its limits.

Returning to the artwork

The original graphics prompted another visit to Toki decades later. In 2023, pixel artist and Zzap! Italia journalist Davide Bottino announced a fan remaster of the C64 conversion. He regarded the original as an above-average conversion whose hero and boss sprites deserved better artwork.9

The project redraws the graphics and adds music by Aldo Chiummo. Its credits recognise reverse engineering, programming, and tools contributed by Mariano Cigliano, Pasquale Sannino, Euan Gamble, Martin Piper, Knight Rider, Oziphantom, and another contributor whose name is withheld. The original conversion’s compromises became material for another group of makers to work on.8

Follow the cartridge and frame routines

Cartridge directory

Sixteen banks behind one window

The cartridge holds 128 KB in sixteen 8 KB banks, and the C64 sees one bank at a time at $8000. Switching on starts bank 0, and by the time the game's first routine in RAM runs $51FB, most of the program has been copied out of the cartridge. During play the game keeps the cartridge out of sight and runs entirely from RAM, including a copy of bank 3 at $8000.

When it needs something from the cartridge, a short routine in the stack page brings bank 0 back, looks the item up in a directory of eight-byte entries, switches to the bank that holds it, and copies it into place $011F. The stage names, GAME OVER, and STAGE CLEAR never reach RAM: they stay in bank 0.

Each directory entry names a bank, where to read in it, where to write in RAM, and how many bytes, and one flag bit decides whether the copy writes into the I/O chips or into the RAM beneath them $0153. Only one entry sets that bit: 120 bytes of colours for colour RAM at $DB48. Switching on copies the first six entries $0100, which lay banks 1 to 4 end to end from $4000 to $BD6B and put two pieces of bank 0 low in memory, then jumps into the copy of bank 3 at $8000 $0118. The last fifteen entries come in five sets of three, each filling $C000, $BD76, and $0C01, one set for each of the five stages.

Starting a stage looks up where its set begins in a five-byte table in bank 0, one byte per stage $0B08, and copies entries from there until it reaches one marked as packed $0A9B. The second flag bit marks that packed entry, 2.2 to 3.3 KB copied to $0C01, and the copy hands it to an unpacker in bank 0 $8DA2. The unpacker works backwards from the end of the data and expands each stage in place to between 5.3 and 7.1 KB, filling $0C01 upward. None of a stage's block is code: the routines that run each of the game's objects read from tables in it, so each stage carries its own data for them $A23D. The same unpacker runs at power-on, where four entries from three banks make one packed block that grows to 14.5 KB at $0C01 $5240. Each stage overwrites that block. Two more entries follow, the same for every stage $0B11. The stage number that picks the set starts at 0 for a new game and goes up by one as each stage ends $81B1.

Timing

A frame driven by an NMI

A CIA timer set to one PAL frame raises an NMI every frame $4D1D, and the NMI sets up a raster interrupt for the top of the status panel $837E. That interrupt picks a display mode the VIC-II shows as black, and behind those eight black lines it switches to the panel's own video bank, character set, and colours. A second interrupt turns the black mode off, and the panel appears $BCE1. The main loop runs the game's updates once a frame $80F9 and spends its spare time waiting for the next one $819B.

The frame

Ten updates, in a fixed order

Each frame the main loop calls ten routines one after another $80F9. The player moves after the objects, the view window is worked out from the player's new position, and the sprite tables are filled just before the background strip for the next scroll step is drawn.

Scrolling follows a zone

Every stage is cut into sections, and each section has a record that says how the view behaves there $92DF. In one setting the view follows the player: while he stays inside a zone the view holds still, and when he leaves it the view scrolls two pixels a frame toward that side $470C. Two other settings in the record make the view move by itself, horizontally or vertically, until it meets the section's limit, and then follow the player along the other axis.

A stage skip behind two joysticks

The player routine also tests for a joystick combination that no player would press by accident $8572. The Discoveries tab says what it does.

09 · Difficulty

Hard in the arcade, easier once the levels are known on the C64

The sources describe the arcade game as hard. Computer and Video Games called it difficult, and almost any attack kills Toki.1 Famitsu's reviewers called the NES version a memorisation game, and the 2018 remake offers difficulty settings and still ends a run on one hit.1,11 The C64 cartridge reads differently. Zzap!64 found the levels fairly big and not hard early on,4 and Commodore Power wrote that once the levels have been sussed they become quite easy, because everything is in the same place.6 The sources read do not say how many players have finished the C64 game.
