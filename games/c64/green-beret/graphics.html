1986 · Imagine Software (Ocean)

Green Beret

Every figure is two sprites, a top half and a bottom half, and the machines at the end of the stages are six or nine. They all draw on 208 shapes kept in the video bank at $4000, and the Harbour's dogs swap ten more in for their fight.

01 · Everything that moves

Every figure, put together from the game's own tables

Each figure here is built from the shapes and colours the game's tables give it, placed as the game places its sprites, and stepped at the game's own rate, fifty frames a second.

The figures are drawn from the listing in the built site.

The hero

His pose is the joystick's bits (left, right, up, down, and fire for a stab), and each pose names where its frames start in player_anim_tops and player_anim_bottoms; a frame lasts six. With the stick at rest he stands in the first frame of his walk. Carrying a weapon changes his top half while he walks and jumps.

The soldiers

Six of the ten types walk, climb and jump through enemy_anim_tops and enemy_anim_bottoms, from the start that the type's base and the direction choose, a frame every five. Each type has a colour for its top half and one for its legs. The ladder frames of the brown soldier and the kicker are drawn faint: neither ever climbs.

Waiting, firing, throwing and dying

The rest set their own shapes. A white soldier and a bazooka man wait lying down, with their legs beside them, and stand up when they move. The crawler alternates two frames, sixteen each. A soldier who fires or throws stops in a pose of his own, and a soldier who is hit flashes white and red for 32 frames before he goes.

Shots and weapons

The six projectiles all flash yellow and red, every four frames, and so do the explosions and the jet, which fly in the projectiles' sprites. The weapons a white soldier drops keep their own colours.

The end of each stage

The lorry is nine sprites and a helicopter six. The dog handler walks as a kicker and stops to turn to the hero. His dogs are shapes the game swaps in over the helicopters' for the Harbour's fight.

The captives

After the Prison Camp the four captives struggle at their posts, their top halves swapping every 16 frames, until the hero passes each of them.

Shapes nothing shows

Of shapes $10 to $DF, 64 bytes each from $4400, and the ten the dogs bring in at $C580, all but fifteen are drawn above. These fifteen are in no table the game reads and no instruction loads: twelve kneeling and leaping soldiers, a small ring and two others, one of them empty.

Every shape and colour is read from the listing: the hero's from player_pose_starts ($25F3), pose_dy and pose_dx ($2559, $2560) and weapon_pose_offsets ($256E); the soldiers' from enemy_anim_starts ($273B), enemy_anim_bases ($278B), type_top_colours and type_bot_colours ($21DF, $21E9); the poses from type_top_shapes ($2207), fire_pose_tops ($30D9), thrower_tops ($30BD), officer_shapes ($30E5), para_top_shapes ($32F8) and dying_shapes ($0A45); the shots from shot_shapes_right ($1BFE), pickup_shapes ($0A4E) and shots_flash_colours ($16DB); the machines from lorry_shapes ($1C8D), rotor_shapes ($1281), handler_shapes ($0D60) and dog_colours ($0FD3); the captives from captive_shapes ($37EE). The sprites' two shared colours, light red and black, are the ones cold_start sets from vic_colours ($3947).

02 · The scenery

Each stage's characters and tiles

The playfield is drawn in characters from one set at $7800, laid out by 64 tiles at $A000, each eight characters square with a colour for each. Each stage brings its own in by swapping blocks of memory.

‹›

Top: the 256 characters at $7800, which the playfield shows, as each stage has them. Bottom: the 64 tiles at $A000, with their colours, as each stage has them. Drawn from the listing with the stage's swaps made as block_swap makes them.

A character with a colour of 8 or more is multicoloured: its pairs of bits pick the background, the grey and light blue every character shares, or its own colour from the low three bits. In play the top four rows of the playfield keep one colour whatever their tiles hold, because the routine that shifts the screen left moves the colours of rows 12 to 23 only. Which swaps each stage makes, and how a column of the map is drawn from two tiles, are on How it works.

03 · The loading picture

The cassette's picture fills in while the tape plays

While it loads, the cassette shows the game's picture: the title, the commando with his rifle, the Imagine and Konami logos, and SHAHID in the corner. It fills in a cell at a time, left to right along each row of cells and down the screen, over 26 seconds, while Martin Galway's loading tune plays (How it works).

The cassette's own bytes, recorded in VICE once the picture was in, and drawn as the video chip draws them. The picture appears at the tape's own pace, where it had got to every fifth of a second.

The screen colours are in place before the first of the picture arrives, so each cell comes in finished over the blue background. The picture is a multicolour bitmap, 8,000 bytes, read in the order it lies in memory: the eight rows of an 8 by 8 cell together, forty cells across, then the next row of cells down. It arrives at about 300 bytes a second, from 72 seconds into the tape to 98.

The disk shows the same picture, which differs from the cassette's in one byte, and plays the same tune, but only once the picture is whole. Then it waits for a key or the fire button before it unpacks the game.
