1986 · Epyx

World Games

The lifter, the diver, the skier, the lumberjacks, the wrestlers, the bull and the caber are all drawn in poses: a short list of sprites, each with its place and the address of its shape. Each event has its own poses and its own copy of the code that places them.

01 · Everything that moves

Every pose of every event, placed by the event's own code

Each event is a program of its own, loaded over the last, with its own poses. Here are all 940 of them, each placed by its event's own routine and drawn in its colours. Choose an event, and point at a pose for what it is.

The poses are drawn by the game's own code in the built site.

Every pose is made from the listing's bytes by the event's own code, run on the kit's 6502 in this page, with the video chip's registers caught as the code writes them. The routines are wl_pose_shapes ($1E3A) and wl_pose_place ($1E8B) in weightlifting, bj_draw_skater ($2394), cd_draw_diver ($21C8), sl_draw_frame ($8EC6), lr_build_sprites ($2445), ct_draw_thrower ($12F6), br_draw_bull ($1E16) and su_pose_right ($1DB6). Where an event changes its shapes during play, the page runs its own routine for that first: cd_sprite_set ($10B3), sl_swap_shapes ($C25E), lr_unpack_set ($2992) and ct_swap_shapes ($1EB2). A pose drawn faint is one that no list in its event shows.

02 · One pose

A pose is a list of sprites

A pose names each sprite's place against the pose's own and the shape it shows. Choose a pose to see its sprites one by one.

The places are counted from the place the page gives the event's routine, which is where the pose stands in play. Each sprite is boxed at its size, numbered as the video chip numbers it; sprite 0 is drawn in front.

In six of the eight events, weightlifting, barrel jumping, cliff diving, slalom, the caber toss and bull riding, a pose is a count of sprites and then, for each, its place from the pose's and the address of its shape. Placing a pose is a loop over them that writes each sprite's place to the video chip and points the sprite at its shape. A shape is 63 bytes, 21 rows of three, and the same six keep the rest of what a sprite needs in the 64th byte, which the chip never reads: the colour in its low four bits, multicolour in bit 7, double width in bit 6 and double height in bit 5 ($1E8B, $23C9). Sumo makes every sprite multicolour and gives each wrestler one colour of its own.

The video chip finds a sprite's shape through a pointer of one byte, so it can only show the 256 shapes in the 16 kilobytes it is looking at, and the screen and its pictures take some of those. The events have more, and each makes room its own way. Weightlifting copies a pose's eight shapes to $4000 each time it shows one, from 206 kept higher up. Bull riding copies its eight to $5400, and turns each row round on the way when the bull faces right, so its frames are stored facing left only. Cliff diving keeps three sets of shapes and copies one to $7000: the dive's, the water's once the diver is in, and the pelican's extra turns. Slalom swaps the falls' shapes in over the push-off's, and the caber toss a second set for walking off. Log rolling keeps its pictures packed and unpacks a set at a time over the last, the standing lumberjacks' to start and a fall's when someone falls.

Sumo and log rolling need more than eight sprites. The two wrestlers are eight sprites each, and the left one is the right one's poses turned round, each shape mirrored as it is copied ($119B); a multiplexer moves sprites down the screen as the beam passes the ones above ($1581). Log rolling sorts its sprites by height ($25D2) and reloads those past the eighth into sprites that have finished, in up to three bands down the screen ($26DF). How one frame of the caber toss is put together, scenery and sprites, is on How it works.
