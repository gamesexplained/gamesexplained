Commodore 64 · 1984 · Epyx

Impossible Mission

Dennis Caswell's stronghold is rebuilt for every game: the map, the robots' behaviour, the password and the puzzle pieces are rolled when a game starts. Two copy-protection checks keep running while you play, and the timing was written for American televisions.

01 · The rooms

All 32 rooms, drawn from their records

Every room in Elvin Atombender's stronghold is a short list of bytes: floors, walls, robots, furniture and lifting platforms.

The room table at $0E70 points at 32 records packed into $0EB0-$1D63. A record starts with four colours (the background, the two shared multicolours and the floor colour), then lists floor segments, gaps in the floor, the two points where the agent can enter, each robot's starting cell, the ball if there is one, the sprite colours, the lifting platforms, and finally the furniture, four bytes a piece: row, column, type and contents.

The game draws a room into a buffer at $C100 while the screen is blanked ($A39C), then reveals it with two black bands that open from the middle of the screen outwards ($A2F7). Furniture comes from a table of 27 types at $232C: a shape, its size and how long it takes to search. The floors alternate between two glyphs, and the walls are one glyph down each side.

02 · The ending

The ending: Elvin's face, lip-synced separately for PAL

Win, and the screen switches to a full-screen picture of Professor Elvin Atombender, who talks while the lights of his control room twinkle.

Drawn from the bytes the game keeps under the I/O area: the glyphs at $D000, the screen at $DC00 and the colours at $F800, in multicolour with the three colours the game sets at $AADF. The mouth is 13 cells; the game swaps them between the glyphs at $AB93 and $ABA0. The emulator's own picture of the same moment is here.

The picture lives where the program cannot easily read it. At start-up the game switches every ROM and chip out of the way and moves three blocks into the RAM beneath them ($71C0): 2 KB of glyphs to $D000, the screen to $DC00 and its colours to $F800. The ending points the video chip at the last 16 KB of memory, where it can see that RAM, and copies the screen and colours into place ($AA40).

The mouth moves in seven steps whose lengths come from a table, and there are two tables. On a PAL machine, the start-up patches three instructions so that the ending reads $AB6E instead of $AB67 ($3FB9). The PAL durations are five sixths of the NTSC ones, because the PAL picture runs at 50 frames a second, not 60, while the speech plays from a timer that runs at the same speed on both.

03 · A new stronghold every game

Puzzle pieces are cut from the picture with masks

Each of the nine puzzles is a random picture, and each piece is that picture seen through two of sixteen stencils.

The puzzle's picture is made the way $B0F3 makes it, and its four pieces are cut by a port of $9C47. The port matches the game's own routine, run in a 6502 simulator, on all 36 pieces of the game in the snapshot this page was made from.

Pressing fire at the start runs $7236, which rolls the whole game:

The room layouts are fixed. Which room sits where, who patrols it and what is hidden in it changes every game.

A piece record is four bytes: the puzzle, a first mask, a second mask and a flags byte. Bits 0 and 1 of the flags say whether each mask is used as it is or inverted, so the four pieces of a puzzle are the picture cut along the first mask in both directions and along the second in both directions. Bits 2 and 3 flip the piece left to right or upside down. The four pieces divide the picture exactly, and only the frame, which every piece carries, is common to them. The pocket computer refuses to drop a piece where any other part of it would land on something already there (IMAGES CAN'T OVERLAP, $8037).

04 · Copy protection

Secret: two protection checks keep running during play

Most protection runs once, while the game loads. This game carries two more checks into the game itself, and each would stop a copy that slipped past the loader.

05 · The pocket computer

Secret: the phone dials real touch tones

The pocket computer's phone key calls for help. The game dials one of two numbers, plays real touch-tone pairs, rings the line and answers.

Each key plays the two frequencies from the table at $7E6E. Sound plays in your browser through Web Audio.

The table holds two SID frequency values per key, one for voice 2 and one for voice 3. Converted at the American NTSC clock they come out as the touch-tone standard (DTMF): rows at 697, 770, 852 and 941 Hz, columns at 1209, 1336 and 1477 Hz. A PAL machine runs its SID 3.7% slower, so a European C64 dials slightly flat. The numbers are in $7E36; 555 is the prefix American films and television use for fictional numbers.

Each call costs two minutes of game time ($7D9D). One number turns the two pieces in the memory window the right way up. The other counts the pieces of the leftmost piece's puzzle and answers A SOLUTION EXISTS when there are four, NEED MORE PIECES when there are fewer ($7FD7).

06 · The robots

Each robot runs a script

The robots' behaviour is a bytecode, interpreted once a frame from the raster interrupt.

Each robot has a pointer into a 256-byte script table at $2112, a current command with a count, and a mode ($8ECC). The commands test which side of the robot the agent is on, whether he is on the same level (within 20 lines) or a random bit, and skip the next command or not. Others jump, wait, turn the robot one step at a time through seven sprite frames, walk it along its platform, or fire the zap. A robot stops where its floor ends ($90FB), so none can walk off a ledge.

The 32 behaviours a game can give a robot are 32 starting points in that table ($2212). This is how one robot ignores the agent while another turns to face him and fires. The floating ball runs a second, simpler language from $2232: wait, step, home in on the agent, loop with a counter ($92CB).

Touching a robot or its zap kills the agent, through the sprite collision register ($9504). The room restarts with the agent at the door and ten minutes gone from the clock ($95E4). A snooze password stops the robots and the ball for 764 frames.

07 · Time

Six hours on the clock, 7 hours 12 minutes on a PAL machine

The mission has until six o'clock, and the clock counts 60 frames to the second.

The clock starts at 12:00:00 and counts in decimal ($83A2). Its seconds are 60 interrupts long, which is a second on an American NTSC machine. A European PAL machine shows 50 frames a second, so there a game second lasts 1.2 real seconds, and the full six hours take 7 hours 12 minutes. The PAL patch at $3FB9 corrects only the ending's lip-sync.

08 · Sound

Sound: the code rooms play a C major scale

Two rooms hold a computer with a big chequered screen. Searching it starts a musical puzzle: find the hidden notes in rising order.

The eight notes of $A9F8, played on three voices an octave apart as $A950 does. At the NTSC clock they are C6 to C7 to within a cent; the second octave of tones is the same scale an octave lower.

The first visit hides two notes among 32 squares. Each success adds a note, up to 14, and pays a password: a lift init for an even number of notes, a snooze for an odd one ($A771). A note lower than the last one pressed starts the count again.

Speech

The voice lines are sampled, about 6 KB in the file words at $E000. A driver at $0880 decodes each short frame of compressed samples into a buffer and plays it by writing volume levels to the SID's master volume register, from a timer interrupt 9,755 times a second ($0C71). While a line plays, the game swaps its raster interrupt for one that only counts frames, so the samples are not disturbed. The data holds eight lines; the game speaks seven of them, and line 6 is never used.
