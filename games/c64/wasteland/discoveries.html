01 · Credits

02 · The Red Ryder

  
Secret: the Red Ryder rifle rolls 200 dice, and the game never hands it out

  
Item 20 in the item table is the Red Ryder, a rifle that takes 7.62mm clips and rolls 200 dice for every hit. The Meson cannon, the gun with the next most, rolls 19. No shop, loot bag, or character in the C64 version holds a Red Ryder, so nobody can fire one without editing the game.

  
The record is in every copy of the item table ($31A8): a price of 12,345, the Rifle skill, the 7.62mm clip, and a clip of 63 rounds, the most a slot's count can hold. With fair dice, 200 of them come to between 600 and 800 in all but one hit in 30,000. The shot widget on Dice and combat lists it, so it can be fired there.

  
The way in is closed at every door. Its stock is 0 in all four item tables on the disks, so no shop offers it ($CC5D-$CC95). No loot bag in the 42 maps names it, and none names its class, 4, from which a bag would draw a random rifle ($919C-$921A). No character a party can meet carries one, and no map's own code calls the routines that put an item in a member's slots.

  
On the PC, players found one. M. Zier's notes on the HQ-Grid's Underground describe a bug in the IBM version's loot bags that hands out items freely, and The best two others are Red Ryders, which are 7.62 rifles with 63 shots, and a range from 600-800 damage, numbers the C64's record matches exactly. Black Raven answered there (16 January 1997) that the bug was the IBM version's alone: it looks like us 64/Apple II users are out of luck. I really wanted those Red Ryders too!

  
The name is Highpool's too. Two of its messages tell of a child who lies dead near his Red Ryder hat, and of Red Ryder himself, who raises his Red Ryder Range Model Air Rifle (with compass in the stock) and fires! (map 10, messages 27 and 31). His is an air rifle; the item table's takes 7.62mm clips.






  
03 · Faran Brygo's computer

  
Secret: the computer in Faran Brygo's hideout is running Wasteland 2

  
A table in Faran Brygo's hideout in Las Vegas holds a computer: You see an Apple //e on the table. The screen says Wasteland 2. You shiver with anticipation. A game called Wasteland 2 came out in 2014, 26 years later.

  
The table is a blocking square at column 1, row 18 of map 39, and walking into it shows the message without changing anything ($3D32). Two squares along, at column 3, another table holds a note: You are about 40% through the game. ($3D34).

  
The fans' notes are about the machine. Posts from Snake Squeezins in September 2002, gathered on the HQ-Grid's Underground, point out that the computer is an Apple //e in the Commodore version as in the Apple's, and an IBM AT on the PC. J. Compton called the Commodore's totally wrong, and L. Mazzei put it down to the C64 version being based on the Apple // version.






  
04 · A wall that talks

  
Secret: the walls under the Temple of Blood spell a sentence, one word a bump

  
The chamber under the Temple of Blood is a sea of blood ringed by 120 squares of mossy wall. Walk into one of them again and again, and it says a sentence a word at a time.

  
Every square of the ring starts as blocking square 3, which shows Moss covered walls rise into the darkness above. and becomes blocking square 4 when it is bumped ($3D8B). Squares 4 to 10 each show a word and hand over to the next ($3D8E-$3DA2, $918F). So eight bumps on one square read the moss, then You've, got, your, balls, to, the, wall., and leave it as blocking square 0, which shows the moss and changes no more. Each of the 120 squares keeps its own place in the sentence. The credits poster in Needles works the same way, but goes round again where the wall stops.

  
Ranger Wrank found it, and told the HQ-Grid's Underground (17 February 1999) to swim out to the wall and run into it a couple of times, the game will give you a message. The ring is the edge of map 28 on the Maps tab.

06 · Damage

  
Bug: an unlucky Ranger's throwing knife can kill anything

  
A shot's damage is the weapon's dice plus the attacker's luck bonus, and below an LK of 9 the bonus is negative. When it takes the sum below 0, the number wraps round to 65,535, and whatever was hit dies. One weapon can get there: a Throwing knife, thrown by a Ranger with an LK of 3 or 4, when both of its dice show 1.

The bonus is (LK − 9) / 2 rounded down below 9, 0 from 9 to 12, and (LK − 12) / 2 from 13 ($A7C3). The lowest attribute a Ranger can be rolled is 3.

  

  
A shot's damage is worked out by one routine ($A7DE). It rolls the weapon's dice ($A7F4), then adds the attacker's luck bonus with two plain additions, the low byte and then the high byte. A negative bonus is held as a 16-bit number whose high byte is $FF, so a roll smaller than the penalty comes out as a number near 65,535. The monster's armour roll takes a few points off that. The rest is more than any monster has: the most is about 40,000, for the Night Terror in Finster's mind maze (map 23), and the subtraction from the monster's hit points leaves it dead ($A6CC-$A6DF). A blow in melee adds the same bonus through the engine's own addition ($07C6), which stops at 0, so a punch can do no damage but never wraps.

  
Only one weapon has dice few enough. An LK of 3 or 4 takes 3 off, so the roll has to be 2: two dice showing 1. Every gun and rocket rolls three dice or more, and explosives leave the luck out, which leaves the Throwing knife's 2d6. With fair dice the knife shows a 1 and a 1 once in 36 throws, and a Throwing knife is used up when it is thrown ($A53A). No loot square in the 42 maps holds one. Christina, who can join the party in downtown Needles, carries one ($40AE).

  
A new Ranger’s attribute roll makes the low LK easier to come by. Of the 7,776 ways its five dice can fall, 261 give an LK of 3 or 4, where the best three of five would give 6.






  
07 · Random numbers

  
Bug: the dice come up doubles one throw in five

  
Two fair dice match one throw in six. Wasteland's match one throw in five, and a pair of ones comes up twice as often as it should. A double means the dice are thrown again, so the bias reaches every check and every shot in the game.

  

    

      
      
      
    

    

      

      

        
– doubles

        

        

        
fewer than fairmore than fair

      

    

    
The first pair of the open throw, first die down and second across. In the emulator, a stub in play threw 4,096 pairs with the game's own routines and the same instructions between the two dice as the throw itself ($084A-$0850), each pair after the seed had been stirred 1 to 256 times, as waiting for a key stirs it. On the simulator, the engine's code threw 2,000,000 pairs on the kit's 6502 simulator from random moments: a random raster line, cycle, timer value, and seed for each.

  

  
The game has no table of random numbers. Each random byte is the sum of three numbers ($24E3): the raster line the video chip is drawing, the low byte of a timer that counts down once a cycle, and the last random byte plus one. A die ($24D6) keeps the byte's lowest three bits, 0 to 7, throws again at once on a 6 or a 7, and adds 1. The raster line and the timer move with the clock, so a byte made a few cycles after another depends on it. After a draw of 7, the die thrown again at once comes out 2 or 3 two times in five, where a fair die would give 2 or 3 one time in three.

  
Every skill or attribute check fails at once on a throw below 5 ($0C73, $0D29). That takes a first pair of 1 and 2 or 1 and 3, one throw in nine with fair dice. The game's dice do it 13.1% of the time on the simulator and 12.9% in the emulator. The checks and shots on Dice and combat give the chances both ways.

  
A single die is uneven too. In 5,000 dice from the Ranger Center's attribute rolls, watched in the emulator, ones came up 1,043 times, fours 727, and sixes 826, where fair dice give 833 of each.






  
08 · Refusals

  
Bug: every NPC is as obedient as the next

  
Each record has four bytes meant to make that character more or less willing: one for using a skill, one for an item, one for an attribute, and one for giving an item away. The check that reads them loses the byte before it is used, so the target is 15 for every NPC. Only Redhawk is different. His trade byte is 255, which is tested first, so he never gives an item away.

Two places in the engine that make a target, from the Source tab. The skill check loads X after the clear; the NPC's check loads it before, and the clear empties it. The attribute check does it the skill check's way ($0D1B-$0D20). In the emulator, booted from the original side 1, the engine's own check ran 256 times with a byte of 10 and a CHR of 20: the target it computed was 15 on every call, where 15 + 5 × 10 is 65.

  

  
The check is $0D61. It reads the byte, refuses at once on 255, and moves the byte into X for $0867, which returns 15 + 5 × X. In between it calls $0795 to clear the target, and that routine leaves 0 in X as well as in A. So a request is refused on a throw under 5, and otherwise when the throw plus one number falls short of 15. For a Use, that number is the NPC's own byte +$31, the hirer's CHR at the moment of the hire ($8D8E). For a trade, it is the CHR of the character taking the item ($0F70). With a CHR of 10 or more only the throw under 5 is left, one request in nine with fair dice and 13.1% with the game's, and that is the same for every NPC, Redhawk's trades aside.

  
The bytes still move. A refusal on a throw under 5 raises the byte by one, up to 10 ($0DA2), and a request carried out lowers it one time in 20 ($0D92). A hire lowers the three Use bytes by one for every 4 points the hirer's side beat the NPC's ($A496). Nothing reads them except the test for 255.

  
The manual promises more. NPCs don't always trade something when you ask them to, it says (page 12), and they have a mind of their own and may not always want to give their items away (page 6). Per Jorner's guide says that they sometimes refuse to trade away equipment or perform an action, and that over time they become less prone to rebellion (page 49). On the C64 the bytes that would do that go up and down, and the check never reads them. The guide is right about Redhawk, who will never trade anything away while conscious (page 52): an NPC who is not conscious is not asked ($0F63).

09 · Creating a character


Bug: the attribute roll that loses its best dice


A new Ranger's attributes are meant to be the best three of five dice. A slip in the sort loses the best dice instead, and the roll averages barely more than three dice would.



Creating a character at the Ranger Center rolls ST, IQ, LK, SP, AGL, DEX, and CHR, then MAXCON as one more roll + 18 ($7FDD). Each roll throws five dice into five slots, the first in slot 4 and the last in slot 0 ($8382). Then an exchange loop moves larger dice up into the higher slots, and the roll adds slots 2, 3, and 4 ($83B0). Sorted properly, those would be the best three of five.



The sort goes down from slot 4 to slot 1. Each pass loads the die in its slot once ($8397) and compares it with every slot below. When a slot holds a larger die, the two change places. The pass should now go on with the larger die it has just taken in. Instead, the branch at $83AB goes back to the compare, so the pass keeps comparing the small die it loaded. A second exchange in the same pass then writes over the larger die that moved up, and the small die ends up in two slots. Thrown in the order 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, the slots end as 1, 1, 1, 1, 5, and the roll is 7 where the best three make 12.

The port was run against the original code on a 6502 simulator for every one of the 7,776 ways five dice can fall, comparison by comparison, and again for 3,000 throws with the game's own dice routine; every slot and every sum matched.





Over all 7,776 throws the roll averages 10.62, barely more than three plain dice at 10.5, where the best three of five would average 13.43. The roll comes out lower than the best three in 72% of throws and is never higher, so a new Ranger's seven attributes lose about 20 points between them on average. Watched in the emulator, 32 rolls at the Ranger Center ended slot for slot as the loop predicts, and 21 of them came out below the best three of their dice.






  
10 · Libraries

  
Bug: a library pays 64 skill points for a seventh level of Energy weapon

  
A library sells a level of a skill for skill points, at a price that doubles with each level. When the price is far above the points, the subtraction wraps round, and the Ranger walks out with more points than before and the level besides.

  
The price of the next level is the skill's base cost, 1, 2, or 3, doubled once for each level already learned, and 255 once that passes 255 ($CBF7). To train, a Ranger needs at least the base cost in points ($CA9C-$CAAB) and a level below the rank ($CAEE-$CAF2). The library then takes the price from the points and refuses only when the remainder has its top bit set, the sign of a negative byte ($CB03-$CB07). A price of 128 against fewer points leaves a remainder with that bit set, and is refused. A price 129 or more above the points wraps past 0 into the positive half again: a price of 192 adds 64 points, and a price of 255 adds 1.

The fans found the 255 first. M. Clem the Hick, on a C64, saw characters come away with 68-71 skill points: the 64 that a price of 192 adds, on top of the 4 to 7 they brought in. It didn't always work, which fits the rank rule: a level must be below the rank before a library sells it.






  
11 · Radiation

  
Bug: armour stops radiation only where the message number is even

  
A radiation square burns every member of the party who is not wearing a rad suit. The code means armour to be no help against it, and on most squares armour is a help after all: the square's message number decides.

The handler for radiation squares ($82E0) shows the square's message, then rolls the square's dice of damage for each member in turn. A member wearing the rad suit, item 41, is passed over. Before the first member the handler sets the flag at $A0 that tells the damage routine to skip the armour roll ($82E7).

  
The damage is taken off through a routine shared with the squares that test the party ($9082). For CON, that routine sets the same flag again, from bit 0 of byte 0 of the square's record ($90AF-$90B4). On a square that tests the party, byte 0 holds flags, and bit 0 set means armour is no help. On a radiation square, byte 0 is the number of the message it shows. So the armour roll, one die for each point of the member's armour class ($A8BB-$A8C5), is skipped where the message number is odd, as the handler meant, and made where it is even.

  
Seven of the twelve records have an even number, among them the 10d6 squares of the robot assembly at Base Cochise. The world map's two records both show message 23, so armour is no help there.

  
In the emulator, the party stepped onto the world map's 5d6 square with every member's armour class set to 30. With message 23 the four members lost 22, 23, 16, and 14 CON. With the record's byte changed to 22, the armour roll was made four times and nobody lost any.






  
12 · A square without a record

  
Bug: the check square that reads the processor port

  
On map 11, the square at column 24, row 13 refuses every step onto it, and says "Ah! Fresh air and open spaces once again."

  
Show the square on map 11 →
Every step onto the square was run in the 6502 simulator with the game's own code ($AD5B): each is refused, and message 53 is shown.

  
The square is class 2, a check, with number 2. Map 11's list of check records holds $0000 for number 2, so the game reads the record from address 0. The C64's first two addresses are the processor's port, not memory. Address 0 reads $2F, the port's direction, and the game takes it for the check's flags. Its bit 7 is clear, so the check runs as the party steps in, and its bit 6 is clear, so there is no test to pass and the step is refused ($8DEC, $8E33). Address 1 reads $35, the port's setting in play, and the game takes it for the message to show: message 53. This square is the only one of the 42 maps whose record is not in the map.

  
The game does the same in the emulator. With the party at column 23, row 13, three presses of L each printed message 53 and left the party, the square, and the clock as they were, and the processor's port read $2F $35 throughout.
