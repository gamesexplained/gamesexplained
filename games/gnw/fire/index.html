1981 · Nintendo

Fire

A 4-bit chip with 65 cells of memory runs the clock, the alarm and both games. Each person on the way from the window to the ambulance is one lit segment, the path moves in four parts that take turns, and a catch is still possible after a person has landed.

01 · The game

Bounce the people to the ambulance

People jump from the window of a burning building at the top left. Two firemen carry a net along the bottom and bounce each one three times, across the screen to the ambulance at the right.

Left: Game A, 30 seconds in. Right: the clock, where the game plays itself once a second. Both are drawn from the segments the chip lit on the kit's model of it, on MAME's drawing of the LCD.

Left and right move the firemen one place, under the left, middle or right of the three bounces. Each catch is a point, and the person flies on to the next place, so a person carried all the way is worth three. One who lands with the firemen elsewhere lies hurt on the ground, and an angel appears at the top right: a miss. The third miss ends the game.

The screen is 67 fixed shapes printed on the LCD, and each one is either on or off. Every place along the path is a shape of its own. To move a person, the game turns one shape off and the next one on.

02 · A replay

One part of the path moves at a time

Step through a minute of Game A, played by a computer player. Each frame is one change on the screen.

Recorded on the kit's Sharp SM5A (kit/gnw/sm5a.js, held to MAME 0.289 instruction for instruction). A frame is kept each time the drawing routine ($0640) finishes a picture that differs from the last. A game starts allowing none on the path, so the first person jumps alone (section 05). The wait is counted in passes of the game's wait loop; 11 passes make a step of about 0.2 seconds.

The game advances in steps, and each step moves one part of the path by one place ($036D): first the middle arc, then the window, the first arc and the way to the ambulance together, then the third arc. Each part therefore moves once every three steps. The smoke over the building moves with the third arc.

03 · Memory and the screen

Every segment is one bit

Click a bit to light its segment, or a segment to find its bit. The picture is drawn from these cells alone.

The cells are the chip's memory as it finished a picture just after the replay above ends. The page turns them into segments with a port of $0640, the game's drawing routine.

The LCD has nine 4-bit latches for each of its two sets of segments. To draw, the game shifts a cell into the latches nine times, copies them, and shifts nine more for the second set ($0640). Twelve cells hold four segments each, and three hold the digits; three more give each digit one extra segment.

So the game never stores where a person is as a number. Each place on the path is one bit, and moving a part of the path means moving every set bit one place on. The chip has no instruction for that, so the game swaps the carry flag with each bit of the part in turn, and every set bit ends up one place further on ($043B to $043E). The firemen, the angels and the smoke move the same way.

04 · The path

Three bounces, four parts

The path has 22 places, in four parts that move separately. A person waits above each net until the next part carries him on.

Each part in its own colour, as the chip's cells list them ($0158, $01C3, $0100, $0180). Game B's lower window, a 23rd place, is shown in grey.

When a person arrives above a net, the game sets a bit for that net ($29). Every press of left or right, and every step, ends by looking at the net where the firemen stand: if its bit is set, that is a catch and a point ($0300). The person stays lit above the net, and the next part of the path carries him on at its next turn. Only when that turn comes with the bit still set is it a miss. So a press that brings the firemen under someone who has already landed still catches him, as long as it comes before that next part moves: two steps later, about 0.4 seconds at the start of a game and 0.25 at the fastest, since the parts move in the order middle arc, first arc, third arc.

05 · Jumps

The window reads the clock and a counter

At each step the game works out whether someone jumps ($00C0). The answer counts on the steps when the first arc moves, if the window's place is empty, and in Game B also when the middle arc moves, for the lower window.

no one on the pathalways

The game counts the people on the path ($09): one more for each jump, one fewer for each who reaches the ambulance and for each miss. Whenever no place on the path is lit, the count is set back to 0 before drawing ($0689).

The limit is the score's tens digit plus one, at most four ($0088). A new game sets it to 0, so the first person jumps alone, the one who jumps because the path is empty. After his first catch the limit is 1, and it climbs back each hundred points: at 100, 200 and so on the tens digit is 0 again and the limit with it.

06 · Misses

A miss stops the game for nine steps

Play the first miss of a game left to itself.

The frames the chip drew from the miss to the hurt figure's going out, on the kit's model.

A miss puts out the person above the net and lights a hurt figure on the ground below it ($03C4). The path then stands still for nine steps ($00DD): at the second the miss's sound plays, at the third an angel appears and the miss is counted ($0197), and at the seventh the hurt figure goes out. The angels light from the right, and the third ends the game with five beeps. A test pad on the board (R2 K1, unpopulated) stops the angel being added if it is held when a miss is counted.

07 · Speed

More people, then faster ones

Move the score to see the limit and the wait that come from it. The same table serves both games ($0088).

ScoreOn the path at mostWaitA step

The bars are the wait, in passes of the wait loop, for every score from 0 to 999; the scale at the left counts passes. A step at the measured length (2,025 cycles at 4 passes, 3,364 at 11, on the kit's model) is about 0.205 seconds at the slowest and 0.124 at the fastest. A person moves every third step.

The wait is 11 passes while the hundreds digit plus the limit is under 4. From 4 it shortens by one pass for each, and from 10 on it stays at 4. So in each hundred the game gets faster through its first thirty points, as the limit grows, and each new hundred starts faster than the last, even though the limit drops back to 1.

08 · Game B

A second window, lower down

Game B on the kit's model, with someone waiting at the lower window.

Game B runs the same path, at the same speed, and adds a window below the first ($01C3). When the middle arc moves and the jump's chance says yes, a person appears there if the lower window and the first arc's last places are empty. He drops into the first arc's last place before the left net when the third arc moves, the first arc's lower places are empty, and either the clock's seconds end in 8 or 9 or an odd number are on the path ($0117). The clock's demo uses the lower window too.

09 · The bonus

At 200 and 500 the angels go

When the score reaches 200 or 500 with an angel showing, the game stops ($005A). No step runs for about six seconds while the angels blink and a short tone sounds twice a second, and then every angel is cleared ($071C). With no angel showing nothing happens. Past 999 the score goes on from 000.

10 · Sound

All the sound comes from switching one pin on and off

Each sound is the chip's own routine run on the kit's model, its pin changes recorded and played back as a square wave.

The buzzer is wired to one of the chip's output pins, R1, which the game sets and clears with ATR in timed loops ($0280, $02B0). A catch gives a short tone, each step that moves someone a quick burst, and a miss a longer tone. The beeps of a miss and the game over each last a sixteenth of a second, one step of the chip's divider ($00A6).

11 · Controls

The keys are added up

Time, Game B, Game A and Alarm are read through one row of the chip's inputs ($0500), and a change counts when two readings in a row agree. The keys held are worth 1 (Time), 2 (Game B), 4 (Game A) and 8 (Alarm), and their sum gives the mode ($0540): 1 the clock, 2 or 3 Game B, 4 to 6 Game A, and 7 or more the alarm's setting. Alarm with anything else counts as Alarm alone. A game starts when its key is let go; while it is held, the score shows that game's best.

12 · The clock and the alarm

The clock plays the game by itself

Play the clock's demo, a step each second.

Once a second the clock runs the whole step at once: the smoke, the jump, and every part of the path ($031E). Its firemen move by themselves toward the part that is moving, once a second ($0340). The alarm is set in its own mode, entered with Alarm; each press that gives that mode turns the alarm on or off, and a bell at the top right shows that it is on. At its minute the ambulance man rings his bell and the buzzer sounds until a key is pressed or the minute ends ($0580). After power on, left and right set the time, an hour and a minute at a time, four times a second while held; in the alarm's setting they set the alarm the same way.

13 · The unit

A Wide Screen game of 1981

Fire first came out in July 1980 in the Silver series, and again on 4 December 1981 in the Wide Screen series as FR-27, the unit this program comes from. MAME's notes call the Wide Screen program a complete rewrite of the Silver one, and FR-27 the last Game & Watch on the Sharp SM5A; the games after it used the SM510 family. In 1989 the Soviet Elektronika company sold the same ROM with different graphics as Kosmicheskiy Most (Space Bridge). The firemen's net appears in Super Smash Bros. Brawl's Flat Zone 2 stage.
