1988 · Ocean

Where Time Stood Still

Every person and creature is a sprite whose frames are stepped by a script, one frame each time the game draws the view.

01 · The cast

Every animation, a frame a pass

An animation is a script in bank 1: a list of frames, each with the step it takes across, down and up. Each class of actor reads one of four lists of scripts, and every pass of the main loop the game moves every actor on by one frame of its script. Pick a class to see its animations play.

Pixel position across ·

 

Each script of the class's list whose frames the class has, played in place from its frame bytes, one frame for every 6.5 frames of the television, the rate the game kept while walking; the steps across, down and up are left out. Paper is white and ink black. A class that shares the people's list shows scripts its creature may never be given, because the game picks a script by what the actor is doing (bank 1 $E2F2). Drawn by this page from the frame lists and pictures in banks 0, 1 and 6; test_cast.js runs the game's own drawing ($A6F8 to $A6F4) on the kit's Z80 for every frame of every class, plain and mirrored, at four pixel positions across and on every row of a character row, and the screen it leaves is the same as this page's, byte for byte. Point at an animation for its script.

The four characters, the tribe and most creatures share one list of 62 scripts at $E454, and a script names frames by number, so the same script draws from each class's own frame list. For the four characters, scripts 0 to 3 are the walks, frames 2 to 5 and 6 to 9, each played mirrored for the other two directions. The tentacle, the pteranodon and the arm in the rock have lists of their own. A script can end in an event when its last frame is reached ($9052).

A frame is six bytes in its class's list: its width, its rows, and the addresses of its picture and its mask ($A493). Most of the people's frames are 16 pixels wide and have no mask. The 16-pixel drawer ($A277) makes one from the picture as it draws, through two tables of 256 bytes, $8100 for a row's left byte and $8200 for its right. They keep the scenery to the left of the pixel before the row's first and to the right of the pixel after its last, and the row between becomes paper, so a figure stands on a silhouette a little wider than itself that keeps it clear of the scenery behind. Where a row starts at the frame's first pixel, the edge pixel before it is carried in from outside the frame, and at pixel position 0 the drawer shifts nothing and that pixel is lost: choose position 0 above to see the left edges thin.

The creatures, and the people's wider frames, are 24 pixels wide and carry a mask of their own, three bytes a row beside three of picture ($A079). Three frames of types $10 to $13 name $8521 as their mask, which is the visibility mask, all set where no scenery stands in front, so with nothing in front those frames draw no paper. Mirrored, a row's bytes swap ends and each is reversed through a table the start-up builds at $8000.

Types $14 to $1B are drawn in two pieces ($A725). Their frame number picks four bytes from a table, $F26A in bank 1 for $14 to $17 and $E2EF in bank 6 for $18 to $1B: the first piece's frame, the step across and down to the second, and the second's frame. Tick "Colour the second piece" above to see where each piece ends.

The tyrannosaur's body is up to 45 rows, and the visibility mask is 48 ($8521 to $85E0). A sprite's first row takes the mask's row numbered by where its top row falls in a character row, 0 to 7, so with its top far enough down the body's last rows read on past the mask into the code at $85E1, and up to four pixels of its feet are not drawn where that code has a 0.

02 · The loading picture

A picture that waits in the dark for its colours

The tape's first fast blocks are the loading picture: the title in rough yellow letters over a man in a hat with a pistol and a woman with a spear, Denton Designs' name above them and Ocean's below. The loader has cleared the screen to black, ink and paper alike, so for 32 seconds the bitmap comes in unseen. The colours follow, and stay off the screen until the last of them is in. Then the whole picture appears at once, 76 seconds into the tape.

The tape's own bytes, at its own pace from the picture's first byte, 39 seconds into the tape. The black and blue stripes are the loader's: it turns the border over at every edge of the tape's signal, so each stripe is one pulse, a thin one a 0 and a thick one a 1. On the tape they go on while the game loads behind the picture; the page stops them once the picture is up.

The bitmap takes 31.9 seconds and the colours 4.7 more, about 190 bytes a second. The picture then stays up, with the stripes still running, for nearly ten minutes, until the last of the tape's blocks is in and the game opens on its control selection screen.
