Driller moves you a fixed step each time its main loop goes round, a pass. On a Spectrum a pass takes from a seventh of a second to nearly two, most of it spent drawing the view, and close to half a second in the middle, so in Amethyst you walk in steps of 250 of the game's units and turn 15 degrees at a time. A Spectrum with a much faster processor would make dozens of passes a second and take a whole step each time. With Fast CPU on, the page plays the game as such a machine would if it were built to stay playable: a new frame 60 times a second, and you move as fast as the Speed sets, whatever is in view.

A pass on each tick, 60 a second

The game keeps its time by the Spectrum's frame interrupt, 50 a second: it takes a second off the clock to the meteor strike every 50 frames, a unit of energy every minute, and fires the laser beacons. Fast CPU counts ticks of its own, 60 a second, in the Spectrum's T-states: 58,333⅓ of them to a tick. With Fast CPU on, each pass starts on a tick, and never more than one pass on the same tick. The pass's own work takes no time, as on a processor fast enough to finish it long before the next tick.

Sixty is the refresh rate of most displays. A Spectrum's screen shows 50 frames a second, and a pass on each of those would show unevenly on a 60 Hz display, one frame in five held for two refreshes. Each refresh of your display, the page runs the passes it owes the game for the time since the last refresh, and shows the screen as the last of them left it in the same refresh, so the screen is always the game's newest. When no key is down the game waits for one, as on a Spectrum, and a key that goes down is taken at once.

A refresh runs at most four passes. A display as slow as 15 Hz still keeps up with the clock, and a refresh that comes late, while the browser was busy elsewhere, starts the game again from where it stopped rather than racing to catch up. Counted in the Spectrum's T-states, the same keys pressed on the same passes make the same game on any display.

The clock keeps absolute time

The interrupt runs in the Spectrum's time with Fast CPU on or off, so everything it counts runs as on a Spectrum, in seconds: the clock, the energy a minute, the laser beacons' shots. So does every wait inside a pass, such as the game's pause after a change of the turn size, and every sound. The processor drives the beeper itself, and while an effect plays the game does nothing else, so the laser's sound, about a fifth of a second, holds the pass that fires as it holds a Spectrum's. The next pass starts on the first tick after. The security question, the control options, the information screen, drilling, the end of a game and the demonstration run at the Spectrum's speed. A minute of play is a minute on either machine.

The Speed sets how far each pass takes you

Fast CPU makes many passes where the Spectrum made one. Anything the game counts in passes would happen that many times as often, so each pass takes only a share of a step. The Speed slider sets how many ticks one Spectrum step takes, K. Each pass walks, turns, looks or rolls 1/K of the Spectrum's step, through the game's own code for a step, and carries the part of a unit left over to the next pass. Holding a key for K ticks moves you exactly one Spectrum step, whatever is on screen. You travel as fast as a Spectrum that made 60/K passes a second, and see 60 frames.

What the game counts in interrupts stays as it is. So at any one Speed you cover the same ground in each second of the clock whichever way you face, where on a Spectrum you cover less while the view is slow to draw. A faster Speed covers more ground before the meteor strikes, as a faster Spectrum would. Moving the slider turns Fast CPU on, and the page remembers the Speed.

The view between two of the game's angles

A share of a turn leaves you at a fractional angle, between two of the game's 72. The game keeps a sine and a cosine for each of its angles, in 64ths. Between two angles the page takes each of them part of the way to the next angle's, rounded to the nearest 64th, and the game builds its view from them with its own code. A walk at a fractional heading goes along that heading. At the game's own angles the screen shows the game's own frame.

Walls stop you as they stop a Spectrum

When a step runs into an object, the game cuts the step short. With Fast CPU each share of a step is cut on its own, so a walk into a wall at an angle could creep along it where a Spectrum stops. In Driller it rarely does.
