## 0.0.296 · 2026-11-02 · Driller (ZX Spectrum) · Aaron Bell

- **Read every object script as words, and sort them by what they pay.**
  Driller keeps its puzzles in short scripts attached to its shapes. The
  manual and the reviews mention none of them, and the largest score in
  the game, 1,000,000 for four shots in the last sector, is one
  instruction in an area's script. Decoding all of the scripts into
  sentences ("shot: hides object 16, shows object 17") and reading them
  as a list found it at once, where reading the interpreter had not. A
  game whose levels carry their own bytecode keeps its secrets in the
  data, so the coverage of the interpreter says nothing about them.
- **Walk the graph of the areas' exits, and look for the ones with none.**
  Every "go to area" instruction is an edge. Built into a graph, Driller's
  showed an area that the edge of the moon leads into and nothing leads
  out of, whose floor drains the probe at every step: a one-way trip no
  source describes. The same graph showed the two sectors whose ways in
  stay closed until a puzzle elsewhere opens them.
- **Run a found puzzle through the port, not through a model of it.** The
  shortest solution came from a search over a hand-written model of five
  shapes' scripts; it was believed only after the same four shots, given
  to the port's own script interpreter, scored the million and destroyed
  the frame.
- **Give the page's clock an interrupt longer than a frame.** Driller's
  interrupt plays a sensor's hit, 44 ms of sound, inside its handler.
  The Play page's machine ran an interrupt at every frame start the clock
  passed, so after a hit it ran the frames it had passed over one after
  another; where a sensor hits every five frames, as in the area off the
  moon's edge, each of those hit again and the tab froze. Every routine
  was in lockstep with the game and the page's own short runs passed: the
  page's pace test, run on a moment from which its planned keys walked
  off the edge, was what hung. Losing the frame starts that pass while
  the handler runs, as the machine does, fixed it, and `test_play.js`
  now gives a sensor a hit at every interrupt (20 hits in 60 frames, one
  every third frame).
- **Measure each routine's own cycles before trusting a fitted pace.**
  The Play page's pace came from a non-negative least-squares fit of
  each routine's cost a call. It held within 6 % on random play and ran
  13 % fast from one moment where held keys looked up into a view full of
  faces. Hooking the machine at every routine's entry and return gave the
  truth: the face sort is a bubble sort that cost 473,000 T-states a call
  there and 14,000 in an ordinary view, and the fit had priced it at
  nothing, putting its cost on the drawing routines it moves with.
  Counting the work inside the heaviest routines and pricing them from
  the measurements, with the routines whose cost a call holds within
  10 % at that cost and the rest fitted around them, brought the 16
  moments of the page's pace test within 4.4 % and 296 of 306 runs of
  60 passes within 5 %. Pricing every routine at its mean a call was
  worse (17 % off on one moment): a routine with a loop of its own has no
  single price.
